\chapter{Optical fiber communications}\label{ch:optical}

This chapter develops the theory behind the optical blocks and shows, block by block, how the
simulator implements it. The treatment follows the two parts of the optical fiber
communications course on which the models are based~\cite{ofcnotes}: intensity modulation with
direct detection (IM/DD) for short and medium reach, and coherent dual-polarization systems
with optical amplification for long haul. The VCSEL follows the rate-equation model of Shi,
Qi, Wang and Hu~\cite{shi2009}. Wherever possible the figures compare the simulated blocks
with the closed-form results they are meant to reproduce.

The code is in four modules: \file{phy/optical.py} (fiber, modulators, amplifiers, detectors,
coherent DSP, free space), \file{phy/lasers.py} (rate-equation lasers), \file{phy/multimode.py}
(multimode fiber) and \file{phy/theory.py} (closed-form references). \Cref{fig:optical-chains}
shows the three families of links they support.

\begin{incode}
Optical signals are complex field envelopes scaled so that $|E|^2$ is the instantaneous optical
power in watts. A single polarization has the shape $(B,n)$ and dual polarization $(B,2,n)$.
Optical blocks keep the following metadata up to date:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \meta{wavelength_nm}, the carrier wavelength;
  \item \meta{power}, the average power in W (launch, after loss, after gain);
  \item \meta{cd_ps_nm} and \meta{cd_slope_ps_nm2}, the accumulated dispersion $D\ell$ and slope $S\ell$;
  \item \meta{ase_psd}, the accumulated ASE density per polarization in W/Hz;
  \item \meta{unit}, $\sqrt{\mathrm W}$ for optical fields.
\end{itemize}
The OSNR can therefore be computed at any point of a link from \meta{power} and
\meta{ase_psd}, without measuring the noise.
\end{incode}

\section{Signals, power and noise}\label{sec:opt-signals}
\subsection{Bandpass signals, analytic signal and complex envelope}
An optical field is a bandpass signal around the laser frequency $f_0\approx193$\,THz:
\begin{equation}
  E_{rb}(t)=a(t)\cos\big(2\pi f_0t+\phi_0(t)\big).
\end{equation}
In amplitude modulation (on--off keying, PAM) only $a(t)$ carries information and the spectrum
is the baseband spectrum of $a(t)$ shifted to $\pm f_0$,
$E(f)=\frac12[A(f-f_0)+A(f+f_0)]$. Phase modulation varies $\phi_0(t)$; a spurious frequency
modulation $\Delta f(t)$ of the transmitter, $E=a\cos(2\pi(f_0+\Delta f(t))t+\phi_0)$, is called
\emph{chirp}. For a real signal $|E(f)|$ is even and $\arg E(f)$ odd, so the negative frequencies
carry no extra information. Two complex representations remove them:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{analytic signal} $E_a(t)=a(t)e^{j\phi_0(t)}e^{j2\pi f_0t}$, with $E_{rb}=\Re\{E_a\}$;
  \item the \emph{complex envelope} $\tilde E(t)=a(t)e^{j\phi_0(t)}=E_R(t)+jE_I(t)$, with
  $E_{rb}=\Re\{\tilde Ee^{j2\pi f_0t}\}$.
\end{itemize}
The spectrum of $\tilde E$ is the positive-frequency part of $E(f)$ moved to $f=0$; it is not
symmetric, because $\tilde E$ is complex. The real signal has power $P_{rb}=\frac12\overline{a^2}$, the analytic
signal and the envelope $P_a=P_{ce}=\overline{a^2}=2P_{rb}$. Following the convention of optical
communications the envelope is normalized so that
\begin{equation}
  P(t)=|\tilde E(t)|^2\quad[\mathrm W],
\end{equation}
which corresponds to the physical field $\Re\{\sqrt2\,\tilde E(t)e^{j2\pi f_0t}\}$. The tilde is dropped from
now on, and $\bar P$ denotes the time average (for OOK with equiprobable bits and $P_0=0$,
$\bar P=P_{\mathrm{peak}}/2$).

\paragraph{Bandwidth.} A real baseband signal of bandwidth $B$ (for example a 10\,Gb/s NRZ drive,
$B\approx10$\,GHz to the first null) produces a bandpass optical signal of bandwidth $2B$ around $f_0$
and a complex envelope of bandwidth $B$ around $0$ (two-sided $2B$). The usual digital signal is
$x(t)=x_{\mathrm{bias}}+\sum_k\alpha_ks(t-kT_s)$, whose power spectral density is
\begin{equation}
  G_x(f)=x_{\mathrm{bias}}^2\delta(f)+\frac{\sigma_\alpha^2}{T_s}|S(f)|^2 .
\end{equation}
Rectangular NRZ pulses give a $\sinc^2$ with first null at the symbol rate $D=1/T_s$; Nyquist
pulses reach the minimum bandwidth $D/2$; raised-cosine pulses with roll-off $\rho$ occupy
$\frac D2(1+\rho)$. QAM modulates the in-phase and quadrature components with two PAM streams,
$\tilde E=a(t)+jb(t)$, doubling the bit rate for the same symbol rate.

\paragraph{Frequency and wavelength.} With $f=c/\lambda$, a small band transforms as
\begin{equation}
  |\Delta f|=\frac{c}{\lambda^2}|\Delta\lambda|,
\end{equation}
so 100\,GHz is 0.8\,nm at 1550\,nm and 0.57\,nm at 1310\,nm. \Cref{tab:bands} lists the optical
bands.

\paragraph{Power levels.} Ratios are given in dB, $G_{\mathrm{dB}}=10\log_{10}(P_1/P_2)$, and absolute
powers in dBm, $P_{\mathrm{dBm}}=10\log_{10}(P/1\,\mathrm{mW})$, so cascades add:
$P_{\mathrm{out,dBm}}=P_{\mathrm{in,dBm}}+G_{\mathrm{dB}}-A_{\mathrm{dB}}$.

\subsection{White Gaussian noise}
A white Gaussian process of power spectral density $N_0/2$ filtered by $H(f)$ has density
$\frac{N_0}2|H(f)|^2$ and power
\begin{equation}
  P_{n}=\frac{N_0}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|H(f)|^2df,
\end{equation}
which is $N_0B$ for an ideal rectangular filter of (one-sided) bandwidth $B$. In the simulator
the noise of a block is generated over the whole simulated band $f_s$ and filtered by the
downstream blocks, so the result does not depend on the sample rate.

\subsection{Polarization and the four quadratures}
A single-mode fiber guides two degenerate modes with the same transverse profile and orthogonal
polarizations. The field is therefore a vector,
\begin{equation}
  \vect E(z,t)=\big(E_{Ix}+jE_{Qx}\big)\hat x+\big(E_{Iy}+jE_{Qy}\big)\hat y,
  \qquad P(z,t)=|E_x|^2+|E_y|^2,
\end{equation}
with four real degrees of freedom, the \emph{quadratures}. The reference polarizations may be
linear, circular or elliptical; birefringence rotates them randomly along the fiber and in time
(minutes in buried cables, milliseconds with vibrations, tens of microseconds on aerial cables),
but it preserves orthogonality and power. Direct-detection systems modulate and detect only
$P(t)$ and are insensitive to polarization; coherent systems modulate and detect all four
quadratures.

\section{The optical fiber}\label{sec:fiber}
\subsection{Structure and modes}
A fiber is a strand of pure silica 125\,\textmu m in diameter. The core has a slightly higher
refractive index than the cladding (by about 0.35\,\%, with germanium doping), and light is
confined by total internal reflection. Solving Maxwell's equations, the field is a sum of guided
modes,
\begin{equation}
  E(\vect r,z,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{modes}}}E_i(z,t)\,\mathcal E_i(\vect r),
\end{equation}
each with its own transverse profile $\mathcal E_i(\vect r)$ and its own propagation constant. A
step-index core guides a single mode (two polarizations) when
\begin{equation}
  V=\frac{2\pi a}{\lambda}\,\mathrm{NA}=\frac{2\pi a}{\lambda}\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{core}}^2-n_{\mathrm{clad}}^2}<2.405,
\end{equation}
which at 1310--1550\,nm needs a core diameter of about 10\,\textmu m. The fundamental LP$_{01}$ mode
has an approximately Gaussian intensity profile. Multimode fibers (50 or 62.5\,\textmu m cores) guide
hundreds of modes travelling at different speeds; they are used with VCSELs inside data
centers (\cref{sec:mmf}). For single-mode fiber the transverse profile is omitted and $E(z,t)$ is
normalized so that $|E(z,t)|^2$ is the power crossing the fiber section.

\subsection{Attenuation}
Absorption and scattering make the field decay as $E(z)=E(0)e^{-\alpha_Ez}$, the power as
$P(z)=P(0)e^{-2\alpha_Ez}=P(0)e^{-\alpha z}$. In decibels the loss is linear in distance,
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{dB}}=10\log_{10}\frac{P(0)}{P(z)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}z,\qquad
  \alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}=20\log_{10}(e)\,\alpha_E=8.686\,\alpha_E=4.343\,\alpha,
\end{equation}
and the link equation of every power budget is
\begin{equation}
  P_{Rx,\mathrm{dBm}}=P_{Tx,\mathrm{dBm}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}L .
\end{equation}
The intrinsic loss of glass combines Rayleigh scattering ($\propto\lambda^{-4}$) at short
wavelengths and infrared vibrational absorption at long wavelengths, plus impurity peaks (the
water peak near 1383\,nm). The minimum, about 0.2\,dB/km (0.17\,dB/km for pure-silica-core fiber,
below 0.18\,dB/km for the best commercial fibers), lies in the C band, where erbium amplifiers
also work. The C band spans 35\,nm, about 4.4\,THz. Across one channel (tens of GHz) the
attenuation is constant: it scales the signal without distorting it. Across a WDM comb it
varies by a few hundredths of a dB/km.

\begin{keypoint}
Throughout the manual $\alpha$ without subscript is the \emph{power} attenuation coefficient
(the NLSE convention); the field coefficient is $\alpha_E=\alpha/2$. Block parameters are in
dB/km. The GN model of \cref{sec:gn} is written with the field coefficient, as in the
literature.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Chromatic dispersion}\label{sec:cd}
The propagation constant $\beta$ depends on frequency, polarization and power. Its frequency
dependence is expanded around the carrier $\omega_0=2\pi f_0$ ($\Delta\omega=\omega-\omega_0$):
\begin{equation}
  \beta(\omega)=\beta_0+\beta_1\Delta\omega+\frac12\beta_2\Delta\omega^2+\frac16\beta_3\Delta\omega^3+\dots,
  \qquad E_{\mathrm{out}}(z,\omega)=E_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega)e^{-j\beta(\omega)z}e^{-\alpha_Ez}.
\end{equation}
The terms have distinct effects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $e^{-j\beta_0z}$ is a constant phase, irrelevant;
  \item $e^{-j\beta_1\Delta\omega z}$ is a linear phase, a delay $t_D=\beta_1z$ (group velocity $v_g=1/\beta_1$),
  which does not distort;
  \item $e^{-j\frac12\beta_2\Delta\omega^2z}$ is quadratic in frequency and distorts: it is
  \emph{chromatic dispersion} (CD).
\end{itemize}
A distortion-free channel needs $|H(f)|$ constant and $\arg H(f)$ linear on the signal band.
Dispersion is an all-pass filter with quadratic phase,
\begin{equation}
  H_F(f)=e^{-j2\pi^2\beta_2(f-f_0)^2z},\qquad |H_F|=1 .
  \label{eq:hf}
\end{equation}
The group delay of a phase-only filter is $\tau_g=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{df}$; for
\cref{eq:hf}
\begin{equation}
  \tau_g(f)=2\pi\beta_2(f-f_0)z,\qquad \frac{d\tau_g}{df}=2\pi\beta_2z,
\end{equation}
so two spectral components $\Delta f$ apart arrive $\Delta\tau_g=2\pi\beta_2\Delta f\,z$ apart:
different colours travel at different speeds. Fiber datasheets give instead the dispersion
parameter $D$, defined from the delay per unit wavelength,
$\tau_g(\lambda)=\tau_g(\lambda_0)+D(\lambda-\lambda_0)z$, so that
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\tau_g=D\,\Delta\lambda\,z,\qquad
  D\Big[\tfrac{\mathrm{ps}}{\mathrm{nm\cdot km}}\Big]=-\frac{2\pi c}{\lambda^2}\,\beta_2\Big[\tfrac{\mathrm{ps}^2}{\mathrm{km}}\Big]
  \approx-0.784\,\beta_2\quad(\lambda=1550\,\mathrm{nm}).
\end{equation}
$D$ and $\beta_2$ have opposite signs; standard fiber has $D\approx+16$--$17$\,ps/(nm\,km)
($\beta_2\approx-21$\,ps$^2$/km) at 1550\,nm and $D=0$ near 1310\,nm. Total dispersion is the sum of
material dispersion (the index of glass varies with $\lambda$) and waveguide dispersion (the
confinement varies with $\lambda/a$); special index profiles shift it (\cref{tab:fibers}).

\paragraph{Example.} A 10\,Gb/s chirp-free NRZ signal has its main spectral content within
$\Delta f\approx R_B=10$\,GHz, that is $\Delta\lambda=0.08$\,nm. After 200\,km of standard fiber
$\Delta\tau=16\times200\times0.08=256$\,ps, 2.6 bit slots: the pulses overlap completely. In a
coherent system at 100\,GBd with roll-off 0.3, $\Delta f=130$\,GHz, and 1000\,km
($\beta_2L=-21\,000$\,ps$^2$) spread each pulse over $2\pi\cdot130\,\mathrm{GHz}\cdot21\,000\,\mathrm{ps^2}\approx17\,000$\,ps, 1700 symbols.

\paragraph{Accumulated dispersion.} The field is linear in the fiber, so cascaded segments
multiply their transfer functions:
$H_{F,\mathrm{tot}}=e^{-j2\pi^2(f-f_0)^2\sum_i\beta_{2,i}L_i}$. Only the accumulated dispersion
\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{acc}}=\sum_mD_mL_m\ [\mathrm{ps/nm}],\qquad \beta_{2,\mathrm{acc}}=\sum_m\beta_{2,m}L_m
\end{equation}
matters, also across optical amplifiers. Dispersion-compensating fiber ($D<0$) can bring
$D_{\mathrm{acc}}$ back to zero; coherent receivers compensate it digitally instead.

\paragraph{Third-order dispersion.} For wide WDM grids, or near the zero-dispersion wavelength,
the term $\frac{\beta_3}6\Delta\omega^3$ matters, with
$\beta_3=\big(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi c}\big)^2(\lambda^2S+2\lambda D)$ from the dispersion slope $S$
(\prm{dispersion_slope_ps_nm2_km}; about 0.057\,ps/(nm$^2$\,km) for G.652 fiber~\cite{g652}). It is 0
by default.

\paragraph{Sign convention.} The simulator follows Agrawal's convention, in which the carrier
is $e^{-j\omega_0t}$ and the linear operator reads $\exp\!\big(j\frac{\beta_2}{2}\omega^2z-\frac\alpha2z\big)$.
The course writes the carrier as $e^{+j\omega_0t}$, which conjugates every phase:
\cref{eq:hf} and the NLSE of \cref{sec:nlse} are the same physics. The dispersion, nonlinear and LO
phases use one convention consistently, so solitons, SPM, CD compensation and frequency offsets
behave correctly. With NumPy's transform a \emph{positive} baseband frequency is then a
\emph{lower} optical frequency, which matters for odd-order terms: with slope, the operator is
$\exp\!\big(j(\tfrac{\beta_2}{2}\omega^2-\tfrac{\beta_3}{6}\omega^3)\ell\big)$ on the NumPy grid and gives
the group delay $\tau=D\ell\,\Delta\lambda+\tfrac{S\ell}{2}\Delta\lambda^2$, which the test suite checks to
0.05\,ps. The accumulated slope is tracked in \meta{cd_slope_ps_nm2} and removed by
\blk{cd_compensation} together with $D\ell$.

\subsection{Polarization-mode dispersion}
Random birefringence rotates the state of polarization and delays the two principal states by
the differential group delay (DGD). Because the birefringence changes along the length, the DGD
is a random variable: Maxwellian, with mean $\langle\tau\rangle=D_{\mathrm{PMD}}\sqrt\ell$, and frequency
dependent (higher-order PMD). LinkStudio uses the coarse-step \emph{waveplate}
model~\cite{marcuse1997}: a span is a cascade of $N$ birefringent sections (\prm{pmd_sections}, 10
by default), each a fixed DGD $\delta\tau$ between Haar-random rotations,
\begin{equation}
  \mat T(\omega)=\mat U_{N+1}\prod_{k=N}^{1}\begin{pmatrix}e^{j\omega\delta\tau/2}&0\\0&e^{-j\omega\delta\tau/2}\end{pmatrix}\mat U_k,
  \qquad \delta\tau=\langle\tau\rangle\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{8N}} .
\end{equation}
The total DGD then satisfies $\langle\tau^2\rangle=N\delta\tau^2$, and the Maxwellian relation
$\langle\tau^2\rangle=\frac{3\pi}{8}\langle\tau\rangle^2$ fixes $\delta\tau$. The rotations come from a QR
decomposition of a complex Gaussian matrix with the phases of the diagonal of $\mat R$ removed.
The test suite extracts the DGD of 4000 fibers from the eigenvalues of $j\,\mat T'\mat T^\dagger$; the mean is
within 1\,\% of $D_{\mathrm{PMD}}\sqrt\ell$ and $\langle\tau^2\rangle/\langle\tau\rangle^2=1.168$ against $3\pi/8=1.178$
(\cref{fig:pmd}).

\subsection{Kerr nonlinearity and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation}\label{sec:nlse}
The refractive index of silica grows with the intensity (Kerr effect), so the propagation
constant depends on the instantaneous power. The complex envelope obeys the nonlinear
Schrödinger equation (NLSE). In the course convention~\cite{ofcnotes},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial E}{\partial z}=j\frac{\beta_2}{2}\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial t^2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}E-j\gamma|E|^2E,
\end{equation}
and in Agrawal's convention, used by the code~\cite{agrawal2019},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial A}{\partial z}=
  \underbrace{-\frac{\alpha}{2}A-j\frac{\beta_2}{2}\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial t^2}}_{\hat D A}
  \;+\;\underbrace{j\gamma|A|^2A}_{\hat N A},
  \label{eq:nlse}
\end{equation}
with power attenuation $\alpha$, group-velocity dispersion $\beta_2$ and Kerr coefficient $\gamma$
(\prm{gamma_w_km}, about 1.3\,W$^{-1}$km$^{-1}$ for standard fiber). The two are complex conjugates of each
other. The linear part has the exact solution
\begin{equation}
  \tilde A(z,\omega)=\tilde A(0,\omega)\exp\!\Big(j\frac{\beta_2}{2}\omega^2z-\frac{\alpha}{2}z\Big),
  \label{eq:dispersion-operator}
\end{equation}
which depends only on the accumulated $D\ell$ tracked in \meta{cd_ps_nm}.

\paragraph{Self-phase modulation.} Without dispersion and loss, \cref{eq:nlse} gives
$A(z,t)=A(0,t)e^{j\gamma P(0,t)z}$: the power modulates its own phase. With loss the power decays as
$P(0,t)e^{-\alpha z}$ and the accumulated phase is
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{\mathrm{NL}}(t)=\gamma P(0,t)\int_0^ze^{-\alpha z'}dz'=\gamma P(0,t)L_{\mathrm{eff}},\qquad
  L_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1-e^{-\alpha z}}{\alpha},
\end{equation}
so the nonlinearity acts over an effective length shorter than $z$, tending to $1/\alpha\approx21.7$\,km
for long spans. SPM chirps the pulse and broadens its spectrum, with a number of peaks that grows
with the peak phase $\phi_{\max}=\gamma P_0L_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (\cref{fig:spm}).

\paragraph{The split-step Fourier method.} With both terms present, \cref{eq:nlse} has no
general closed form. Formally $A(z+h)=e^{h(\hat D+\hat N)}A(z)$; the two operators do not commute,
but each is trivial on its own: $\hat D$ is a multiplication in the frequency domain, $\hat N$ in
the time domain. The symmetric (Strang) splitting
\begin{equation}
  A(z+h)=e^{\frac h2\hat D}\,e^{h\hat N}\,e^{\frac h2\hat D}A(z)+\mathcal O(h^3)
  \label{eq:strang}
\end{equation}
is exact to second order, by the Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula (the first commutator cancels
by symmetry); over a fixed length the global error is $\mathcal O(h^2)$. Attenuation and any
distributed gain enter the half steps. The nonlinear phase is applied at mid-step with the
effective length of the local net loss $a=\alpha-g$,
\begin{equation}
  L_{\mathrm{eff}}(h)=\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}e^{-az'}dz'=\frac{2\sinh(ah/2)}{a} .
\end{equation}
For dual polarization the Manakov equation replaces $|A|^2$ by $|A_x|^2+|A_y|^2$ and $\gamma$ by
$\frac89\gamma$, the average over fast random polarization evolution.

\paragraph{Choosing the step.} A fixed step suited to 0\,dBm per channel, where the nonlinear
length $1/(\gamma P)$ is hundreds of km, is far too long for the 28\,dBm soliton of
\cref{fig:soliton}, whose nonlinear length is about 1\,km; a fixed 2\,km step leaves a 12\,\% error
there. The solver therefore uses the nonlinear-phase-rotation criterion of Sinkin et
al.~\cite{sinkin2003}: each step is limited so that the peak nonlinear phase
$\gamma P_{\mathrm{peak}}L_{\mathrm{eff}}$ stays below \prm{nonlinear_phase_rad} (0.05\,rad by default), and never
exceeds $1/$\prm{steps_per_km}. The step lengthens automatically as the power decays. With the
defaults the soliton error falls to $3\times10^{-4}$, while WDM links keep 1--2\,km steps. Without
\prm{nonlinear} the solver applies one exact FFT for the whole span.

\section{Semiconductor lasers}\label{sec:lasers}
\subsection{Absorption, stimulated and spontaneous emission}
Lasers, photodiodes and amplifiers are quantum systems with (at least) two energy levels.
Three processes occur, with probabilities that depend on the conditions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{absorption}: a photon of energy $hf=E_2-E_1$ lifts a carrier to the upper
  level and disappears (the principle of photodiodes);
  \item \textbf{stimulated emission}: a photon makes an excited carrier drop and creates a second
  photon with the same frequency, phase, polarization and direction, coherently amplifying the
  field (the principle of lasers and amplifiers); it dominates absorption only with a population
  inversion, obtained by pumping;
  \item \textbf{spontaneous emission}: an excited carrier drops by itself and emits a photon with
  random phase and direction, the source of optical noise.
\end{itemize}
In a semiconductor laser a forward current injects carriers into the active region of a p-n
junction; spontaneous emission starts the process, stimulated emission amplifies it, and the
optical feedback of the cavity (cleaved facets, a distributed Bragg grating in DFB and DBR lasers,
an external filter in ECLs) acts as a narrow filter $|H(f)|^2$ on the broad gain spectrum, so that
emission occurs at a single frequency $f_c$. Fabry--Perot lasers, with several longitudinal modes,
are not used for high-quality links.

\subsection{The CW spectrum: relative intensity noise and phase noise}
Ideally a laser in continuous-wave (CW) operation emits $E(t)=a\,e^{j\phi_0}$, a line at $f_0$. Real
lasers have
\begin{equation}
  E(t)=\big(a+n_{\mathrm{RIN}}(t)\big)e^{j\phi_0(t)},
\end{equation}
with relative intensity noise (RIN) on the amplitude and phase noise on the phase. In good
lasers the line shape is dominated by phase noise and specified by its full width at half
maximum, the \emph{linewidth} $\Delta\nu$. The simplest model makes the instantaneous frequency a
white Gaussian process, so the phase is a Wiener process (\cref{eq:wiener}),
\begin{equation}
  \phi_k=\phi_{k-1}+\N\Big(0,\frac{2\pi\Delta\nu}{f_s}\Big),
\end{equation}
which produces a Lorentzian spectrum; an additional $1/f$ (flicker) frequency noise would make
it Gaussian. Typical values: DFB lasers 10--20\,dBm with linewidths of a few MHz, external-cavity
lasers 100--300\,kHz (below 100\,kHz for coherent transceivers, $\delta f/f_0\approx10^{-9}$), special
lasers for metrology and quantum communications 1--10\,Hz. RIN is the power noise referred to the
average power, $\sigma_P^2=\mathrm{RIN}\cdot\bar P^2\cdot B$; the blocks add it as Gaussian noise over
the simulated bandwidth $f_s/2$, as in the IEEE~802.3 link model~\cite{cunningham2002}.
\codeexcerpt{wiener}{Wiener phase noise of a laser of linewidth $\Delta\nu$.}
\codeexcerpt{rin}{Relative intensity noise referred to the average power.}

\subsection{The rate equations}
A single-mode laser is described by the number $N$ of carriers in the active region, the number
$S$ of photons in the lasing mode and the optical phase $\phi$~\cite{coldren2012}:
\begin{align}
  \frac{dN}{dt}&=\frac{I-I_l}{q}-\frac{N}{\tau_n}-G(S)\,(N-N_T)\,S,\\
  \frac{dS}{dt}&=\beta\frac{N}{\tau_n}+G(S)\,(N-N_T)\,S-\frac{S}{\tau_p},\qquad G(S)=\frac{g_0}{1+\epsilon S},\\
  \frac{d\phi}{dt}&=\frac{\alpha_H}{2}\Big(g_0(N-N_T)-\frac1{\tau_p}\Big).
  \label{eq:rate}
\end{align}
The terms are: injection of the current $I$ minus a leakage $I_l$; carrier recombination with
lifetime $\tau_n$; stimulated emission with gain coefficient $g_0$ above the transparency carrier
number $N_T$, compressed by $\epsilon$ at high photon numbers; the fraction $\beta$ of spontaneous
emission that falls into the lasing mode; photon loss through the mirrors and absorption with
lifetime $\tau_p$. The output power is the emitted share $\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}$ of the photon loss rate,
\begin{equation}
  P=\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}\,\frac{hf\,S}{\tau_p},
\end{equation}
and the phase equation, with the linewidth-enhancement (Henry) factor $\alpha_H$, expresses that a
change of carrier density changes the refractive index together with the gain. All quantities are
numbers rather than densities, as in the circuit models of
VCSELs~\cite{mena1999,shi2009}.

\paragraph{Steady state and the P-I curve.} With $dN/dt=dS/dt=0$, $\beta=0$ and $\epsilon=0$, the
photon equation either has $S=0$ or clamps the gain at the losses, $g_0(N-N_T)=1/\tau_p$. Above
threshold the carrier number stays at
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{th}}=N_T+\frac1{g_0\tau_p},\qquad I_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{qN_{\mathrm{th}}}{\tau_n}+I_l,
\end{equation}
and every additional carrier becomes a photon:
\begin{equation}
  P=\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}\frac{hf}{q}\,(I-I_{\mathrm{th}}).
\end{equation}
This is the linear P-I curve of \cref{fig:vcsel-li}: below threshold the laser behaves like an LED
(spontaneous emission only), above it the slope efficiency is $\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}hf/q$ (W/A), and at high
current gain compression and heating bend it down. With $\beta>0$ the threshold is soft. The test
suite checks the threshold and the slope exactly.

In general (with $\beta$, $\epsilon$ and the spatial terms of \cref{sec:vcsel}) the steady state is found
from the photon number: for a given $S$ the two stationary equations for the carriers are linear,
so $N$ follows in closed form, and the current that sustains it is
$I=q\big(N/\tau_n+G(S)(N-N_T)S\big)+I_l$. This current grows monotonically with $S$, and a bisection on
$\ln S$ inverts it.
\codeexcerpt{laser_steady}{Carriers that hold the photon number constant.}

\paragraph{Large-signal response.} The blocks integrate \cref{eq:rate} in time with fourth-order
Runge--Kutta, using $\ln S$ instead of $S$ (the photon number spans many decades between below and
above threshold) and sub-steps no longer than $\tau_p$. The drive current is linearly interpolated
between samples. Each frame starts from the static state; before the frame, the integrator runs
over its last $3\tau_n$, so that it starts in the state in which it ends, as the circular FFTs
downstream require. When numba is installed the loop is compiled (about 20\,ms per frame of a few
thousand samples); otherwise the same code runs in NumPy.
\codeexcerpt{laser_rhs}{Right-hand side of the rate equations (single mode and VCSEL).}

\paragraph{Small-signal response.} Around a bias point the rate equations can be linearized,
$\dot{\vect x}=\mat A\vect x+\vect b\,\delta I$, with $\vect x$ the deviations of the carriers and photons,
$\mat A$ the Jacobian and $\vect b=(1/q,0,0)\T$. The modulation response is
\begin{equation}
  H(j\omega)=\frac{\vect e_S\T(j\omega\mat I-\mat A)^{-1}\vect b}{\vect e_S\T(-\mat A)^{-1}\vect b}.
\end{equation}
For the single-mode laser it reduces to the classic damped resonance
\begin{gather}
  H(f)\approx\frac{f_r^2}{f_r^2-f^2+jf\gamma/(2\pi)},\qquad
  (2\pi f_r)^2\approx\frac{g_0S}{\tau_p(1+\epsilon S)},\\
  \gamma\approx Kf_r^2+\frac1{\tau_n},\qquad K=4\pi^2\Big(\tau_p+\frac{\epsilon}{g_0}\Big),
\end{gather}
so the relaxation frequency grows with the square root of the photon number (of $I-I_{\mathrm{th}}$), the
damping grows with $f_r^2$ (the K-factor), and the bandwidth saturates. The RC parasitics of the
chip add a pole, $1/(1+jf/f_p)$ (\prm{parasitic_ghz}), applied to the drive current. This is the
response the inspector plots for \blk{dml} and \blk{vcsel}.
\codeexcerpt{laser_small_signal}{Small-signal response from the linearized rate equations.}

\subsection{Chirp of a directly modulated laser}\label{sec:chirp}
Changing the current changes the carrier density, hence the index and the optical phase (and
the temperature, on longer time scales). The phase equation \cref{eq:rate} gives the frequency
deviation. Using the photon equation to eliminate the gain and neglecting spontaneous emission,
$g_0(N-N_T)-1/\tau_p\approx d\ln S/dt+\epsilon S/\tau_p$, hence
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\nu(t)=\frac{\alpha_H}{4\pi}\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\ln P(t)+\kappa P(t)\Big),\qquad
  \kappa=\frac{\epsilon}{\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}hf},
\end{equation}
the Koch--Bowers relation~\cite{koch1984}. The first term, the \emph{transient} chirp, follows the
edges of the power; the second, the \emph{adiabatic} chirp, is a frequency offset proportional to
the power, $\alpha_H\kappa/(4\pi)$, about 1.1\,GHz/mW for the default DFB. In the block the carrier
frequency is defined at the mean current, so the adiabatic term moves the "1" and "0" levels to
opposite sides of the carrier (\cref{fig:dml-chirp}). The test suite checks the adiabatic step
against $\frac{\alpha_H}{4\pi}\epsilon(S_1-S_0)/\tau_p$ to 2\,\%.

A bit-synchronous phase modulation broadens the optical spectrum considerably, so a directly
modulated laser (DML) is far more sensitive to chromatic dispersion than an external modulator.
This is why low-cost high-rate DMLs work in the O band, near the zero-dispersion wavelength: the
higher loss and the lack of amplifiers do not matter over 2--40\,km, and data-center links use
millions of them. The \emph{56\,Gb/s PAM-4 with a directly modulated laser} scenario shows it:
error-free over 10\,km at 1310\,nm, closed within 2\,km at 1550\,nm.

\subsection{The directly modulated laser block}
\blk{dml} turns the normalized drive $x\in[-1,1]$ into the current
$I=I_{\mathrm{bias}}+I_{\mathrm{mod}}x$, passes it through the parasitic pole and integrates the rate
equations. The defaults describe a 25G-class O-band DFB: threshold 10\,mA, 3-dB bandwidth about
28\,GHz at 60\,mA, $\alpha_H=4$. The metrics are the threshold current, the average power and the
static extinction ratio $P(I_{\mathrm{bias}}+I_{\mathrm{mod}})/P(I_{\mathrm{bias}}-I_{\mathrm{mod}})$. Directly modulated lasers
have low extinction ratios (below 8\,dB, 3--4\,dB for the fastest), which costs sensitivity
(\cref{sec:imdd-penalties}); their PAM levels are characterized by the outer optical modulation
amplitude, $\mathrm{OMA}=P_{\mathrm{high}}-P_{\mathrm{low}}$. For example, a PAM-4 DML with levels 1, 2.5, 4 and 5.5\,mW has
$\bar P=3.25$\,mW (5.1\,dBm), $\mathrm{ER}=7.4$\,dB and $\mathrm{OMA}=4.5$\,mW. The best DMLs reach 40--50\,GBd and
cost an order of magnitude less than externally modulated transmitters.
\codeexcerpt{laser_block}{Drive current, rate equations and noise of the laser blocks.}

\begin{pitfall}[What the laser blocks leave out]
The rate equations are deterministic: RIN and linewidth are added as separate noise terms with
the measured values instead of Langevin forces, and the linewidth is not tied to $\alpha_H$ and
$\beta$. The DFB has no thermal model (a TEC holds its temperature), so the thermal part of the chirp
is absent. Mode competition between longitudinal modes is not modelled.
\end{pitfall}

\section{The VCSEL}\label{sec:vcsel}
A vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL) has its cavity along the growth direction,
between two distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs), with an oxide aperture that confines current and
light. The cavity is so short that only one longitudinal mode fits; the planar structure gives a
circular beam that couples well into fiber, allows on-wafer testing and two-dimensional arrays,
and the small volume gives a high modulation bandwidth at low current. VCSELs at 850\,nm are the
cheapest lasers for multimode data-center links. Two physical effects shape their behaviour and
are missing from the single-mode rate equations~\cite{shi2009}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{self-heating}: poor heat dissipation and the resistance of the DBRs heat the
  device, raising the threshold and rolling the output power over at high current;
  \item \textbf{spatial effects}: the transverse profiles of carriers and photons differ; the
  lasing mode depletes the carriers at the centre (spatial hole burning) and lateral diffusion
  refills them, which causes mode competition and secondary pulsations at turn-off.
\end{itemize}
\blk{vcsel} implements the circuit-level model of Shi, Qi, Wang and Hu~\cite{shi2009}, built on the
spatially independent rate equations of Morikuni et al.~\cite{morikuni1999} and the
comprehensive model of Mena et al.~\cite{mena1999}.

\subsection{Spatially dependent rate equations}
Letting the carrier and photon numbers depend on the radial position $r$ in the active disc of
radius $R$,
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial N(r,t)}{\partial t}&=\frac{I(r,t)}{q}-\frac{N(r,t)}{\tau_n}-G(r,t)S(r,t),\\
  \frac{\partial S(r,t)}{\partial t}&=\beta\frac{N(r,t)}{\tau_n}+G(r,t)S(r,t)-\frac{S(r,t)}{\tau_s},\qquad
  G(r,t)=\frac{G_0\big[N(r,t)-N_T\big]}{1+\epsilon S_0},
\end{align}
with $S_0$ the magnitude of the photon distribution and $\tau_s$ the photon lifetime. Solving these
needs a discretization in space and time, too heavy for system simulation.

\subsection{Bessel expansion and the reduced equations}
With cylindrical symmetry the carrier profile is expanded in the eigenfunctions of the disc and
the photon profile is fixed:
\begin{equation}
  N(r,t)=N_0(t)-\sum_{i\ge1}N_i(t)\,J_0\Big(\frac{\sigma_ir}{R}\Big),\qquad S(r,t)=S_0(t)\,\psi(r),
\end{equation}
where $J_0$ is the Bessel function of order zero and $\sigma_i$ the $i$-th root of $J_1$
($\sigma_0=0$, $\sigma_1=3.8317$, so that the radial carrier flux vanishes at the edge). The functions
are orthogonal on the disc,
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^RJ_0\Big(\frac{\sigma_mr}{R}\Big)J_0\Big(\frac{\sigma_nr}{R}\Big)r\,dr=
  \begin{cases}0 & m\ne n\\ \frac{R^2}{2}J_0^2(\sigma_m) & m=n,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
so projecting the carrier equation on each term gives one ordinary equation per coefficient.
Keeping two terms ($N_0$, the mean carrier number, and $N_1$, the depth of the hole burnt by the
mode) and a uniform current leaves three equations:
\begin{align}
  \frac{dN_0}{dt}&=\frac{I-I_l}{q}-\frac{N_0}{\tau_n}-\frac{G_0}{1+\epsilon S_0}\big[(N_0-N_T)h_0-N_1h_1\big]S_0,\\
  \frac{dN_1}{dt}&=\frac{G_0}{1+\epsilon S_0}\big[(N_0-N_T)h_2-N_1h_3\big]S_0-\frac{N_1}{\tau_n}(1+h),\\
  \frac{dS_0}{dt}&=\beta\frac{N_0}{\tau_n}+\frac{G_0}{1+\epsilon S_0}\big[(N_0-N_T)h_0-N_1h_1\big]S_0-\frac{S_0}{\tau_s}.
\end{align}
Lateral diffusion with coefficient $D$ turns into the extra decay $h/\tau_n$ of the hole, with
$h=(\sigma_1L_D/R)^2$ and the diffusion length $L_D=\sqrt{D\tau_n}$. The coefficients $h_i$ are overlap
integrals between the photon and carrier distributions:
\begin{gather}
  h_0=\frac{2}{R^2}\int_0^R\psi(r)\,r\,dr,\qquad
  h_1=\frac{2}{R^2}\int_0^R\psi(r)J_0\Big(\frac{\sigma_1r}{R}\Big)r\,dr,\\
  h_2=\frac{2}{R^2J_0^2(\sigma_1)}\int_0^R\psi(r)J_0\Big(\frac{\sigma_1r}{R}\Big)r\,dr,\qquad
  h_3=\frac{2}{R^2J_0^2(\sigma_1)}\int_0^R\psi(r)J_0^2\Big(\frac{\sigma_1r}{R}\Big)r\,dr,
\end{gather}
with $J_0(\sigma_1)=-0.4028$. For the Gaussian mode (zero-order Laguerre--Gauss beam of waist $R_m$)
\begin{equation}
  \psi(r)=2\Big(\frac{R}{R_m}\Big)^2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2r^2}{R_m^2}\Big),\qquad h_0=1-e^{-2R^2/R_m^2},
\end{equation}
and the other integrals are evaluated numerically. For the default device ($R=3.5$\,\textmu m,
$R_m=2.5$\,\textmu m, $L_D=1.5$\,\textmu m) $h_0=\vcselhzero$, $h_1=\vcselhone$, $h_2=\vcselhtwo$,
$h_3=\vcselhthree$ and $h=\vcselhd$. With $h_1=h_2=h_3=0$ and $h_0=1$ the equations reduce to the single-mode
laser of \cref{sec:lasers}, which is how the code handles both. The test suite checks the closed form
of $h_0$ and the narrow-mode limits $h_1\to1-\sigma_1^2R_m^2/(8R^2)$, $h_3\to h_2$.
\codeexcerpt{laser_overlap}{Overlap integrals of the Gaussian mode with the Bessel modes.}

\paragraph{Turn-off transient.} When the current drops below threshold the photons disappear in
a few photon lifetimes, but the carriers lost to hole burning are then refilled by diffusion
from the edges of the disc. The carrier number in the mode region momentarily exceeds threshold
again and the laser emits a secondary pulse. \Cref{fig:vcsel-transient} reproduces the square-wave
experiment of the paper (0.5 and 5\,mA): with hole burning the light lingers and rings after the
falling edge, while the uniform model switches off at once. The test suite checks that the
emitted energy after turn-off is larger with hole burning.

\subsection{Tucker's thermal model}
As the device heats up, the bandgap of the active layer shrinks and the active region no longer
confines the carriers: a thermal leakage current, modelled as a polynomial in temperature,
\begin{equation}
  I_l(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iT^{\,i-1},
\end{equation}
is lost from the injection. The power dissipated as heat is the electrical power minus the light,
$\Delta P=VI-P_L$, and the junction temperature follows the thermal rate equation
\begin{equation}
  T=T_0+R_{\mathrm{th}}\Delta P-\tau_{\mathrm{th}}\frac{dT}{dt},
\end{equation}
with ambient temperature $T_0$, thermal impedance $R_{\mathrm{th}}$ and thermal time constant
$\tau_{\mathrm{th}}\approx1$\,\textmu s. The electrical characteristic is that of a diode with series resistance,
\begin{equation}
  V=nV_T\ln\Big(\frac{I}{I_{ss}}+1\Big)+IR_s,\qquad R_s(T)=\frac{c_1}{T-c_2},\qquad I_{ss}(T)=b_1I_{ss0}(T-b_2),
\end{equation}
with ideality factor $n$, thermal voltage $V_T=kT/q$ and temperature-dependent series resistance
and saturation current. In a SPICE implementation the diode, the photon storage ($C_{ph}=qS_c$) and
loss ($R_{ph}=\tau_{ph}/C_{ph}$) become circuit elements~\cite{shi2009}; LinkStudio solves the same
equations directly.
\codeexcerpt{vcsel_vi}{Diode characteristic with temperature-dependent parameters.}

\begin{incode}
The thermal time constant is three orders of magnitude longer than a frame (a few ns), so the
temperature cannot follow the bits: it settles at the operating point set by the mean current.
\blk{vcsel} solves $T=T_0+R_{\mathrm{th}}\big(V(I,T)I-P(I-I_l(T))\big)$ at the bias current by damped
fixed-point iteration, subtracts the resulting leakage from the drive and reports the junction
temperature as a metric. The same solver at every current gives the static L-I curves
(\code{light_current}).
\end{incode}

\begin{pitfall}[VCSEL parameters]
Shi et al.\ take their parameters from Mena et al.~\cite{mena1999} without listing them. The
defaults of \blk{vcsel} are a self-consistent 25G-class 850\,nm device: carrier and photon numbers
from an aperture of 7\,\textmu m and three quantum wells, gain coefficient chosen for a 15\,GHz relaxation
frequency at 7\,mA, slope efficiency 0.55\,W/A, and a cubic leakage polynomial
($I_l=2\times10^{-9}T^3$\,A, $T$ in °C) that reproduces the qualitative behaviour of the paper. Fit
the parameters to measured L-I and S$_{21}$ curves before drawing quantitative conclusions. The model
also keeps a single transverse mode for the photons; the multimode spectrum enters only through
\prm{spectral_width_nm} in the multimode fiber.
\end{pitfall}

\section{External modulators}\label{sec:modulators}
\subsection{External modulation}
In external modulation the laser runs in CW at constant current, emitting
$E_{CW}(t)=\sqrt{P_{CW}}\,e^{j\phi_0}$, and a separate device driven by the electrical signal $V_{\mathrm{mod}}(t)$
imposes the modulation,
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{mod}}(t)=\sqrt{P_{CW}}\,e^{j\phi_0}\,f\big(V_{\mathrm{mod}}(t)\big).
\end{equation}
Most modulators exploit a voltage-dependent refractive index. In a lithium-niobate (LiNbO$_3$)
waveguide the electro-optic effect makes the phase linear in the applied voltage,
$E_{\mathrm{out}}=E_{\mathrm{in}}e^{jkV}$: a few centimetres of waveguide are an almost perfect phase modulator.

\subsection{The Mach--Zehnder modulator}
Two such phase modulators in the arms of a Mach--Zehnder interferometer, with 3-dB couplers,
give
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{in}}}{2}\big(e^{jkV_1}+e^{jkV_2}\big)
  =E_{\mathrm{in}}\,e^{jk\frac{V_1+V_2}{2}}\cos\Big(k\frac{V_1-V_2}{2}\Big):
  \label{eq:mzm}
\end{equation}
the difference voltage modulates the amplitude, the sum voltage the phase. In \emph{push-pull}
operation ($V_1=-V_2=V/2$) the phase term vanishes and the modulation is purely amplitude,
\emph{chirp-free}. The voltage that moves the output from minimum to maximum is the switching or
half-wave voltage $V_\pi$ ($k=\pi/V_\pi$). With a static bias that shifts one arm by $-\pi/2$ relative
to the other, the null is at $V=0$ and the characteristic is the sine law
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{out}}=E_{\mathrm{in}}\sin\Big(\frac\pi2\frac{V}{V_\pi}\Big),\qquad
  P_{\mathrm{out}}=P_{\mathrm{in}}\sin^2\Big(\frac\pi2\frac{V}{V_\pi}\Big)=\frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}}{2}\Big[1-\cos\Big(\pi\frac{V}{V_\pi}\Big)\Big]
\end{equation}
(\cref{fig:mzm}). The field characteristic is odd and spans $\pm E_{\mathrm{in}}$ for $V\in[-V_\pi,V_\pi]$: biased at
null it modulates the field (coherent formats). The power characteristic is a raised cosine:
biased at quadrature ($V_\pi/2$) with a swing of $\pm V_\pi/2$ it goes from null to peak, as needed for
OOK and PAM-M. The drive of an OOK transmitter is
\begin{equation}
  V(t)=V_{\mathrm{bias}}+\frac{V_\pi}{2}\sum_k\alpha_ks(t-kT_s),\qquad \alpha_k=\pm1,\ \max s(t)=1,
\end{equation}
and the average output power is $(P_{\mathrm{out}}^1+P_{\mathrm{out}}^0)/2$. For PAM-4 the four voltage levels are
placed on the $\sin^2$ curve so that the powers are equally spaced:
$V_i=\frac{2V_\pi}{\pi}\arcsin\sqrt{P_i/P_{\mathrm{in}}}$.

\paragraph{Parameters.} Commercial lithium-niobate MZMs have $V_\pi=3$--5\,V, extinction ratios
$\mathrm{ER}=P_{\mathrm{out}}^1/P_{\mathrm{out}}^0$ of 15--20\,dB (up to 30\,dB), electro-optic bandwidths of tens of GHz (60--100\,GHz
for the best) and insertion losses $L$ of 3--5\,dB, so $P_{\mathrm{out,dBm}}^1=P_{\mathrm{laser,dBm}}-L_{\mathrm{dB}}$. A 45\,mW
(16.5\,dBm) DFB, an MZM with 4\,dB loss and full-swing OOK give an average of
$16.5-4-3=9.5$\,dBm. The extinction ratio is finite because the arms are never exactly balanced.
The electrodes, package and driver limit the bandwidth; their effect is an equivalent
electrical response $H_{\mathrm{mod}}(f)$ applied to the drive before the electro-optic conversion. MZMs
cost around a thousand euros, largely because of the drivers.

\paragraph{Electro-absorption modulators.} An EAM absorbs light below a cutoff wavelength that an
applied voltage shifts (Franz--Keldysh effect, quantum-confined Stark effect). It is almost
chirp-free, can be integrated with a DFB laser (EML) and is common for 20--40\,km links. It is
modelled by \blk{intensity_modulator}.

\subsection{The modulator blocks}
\blk{mach_zehnder_modulator} combines a CW laser (power, linewidth, RIN) with an MZM. The drive
$x\in[-1,1]$, filtered by a first-order $H_{\mathrm{mod}}$ (\prm{bandwidth_ghz}), sets
$V=V_{\mathrm{bias}}+V_{\mathrm{swing}}x$, with quadrature bias and $V_\pi/2$ swing by default. A finite extinction ratio
comes from unequal arm amplitudes $(1\pm\delta)/2$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{E_{\mathrm{out}}}{E_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac1j\Big(\frac{1+\delta}{2}e^{j\theta}-\frac{1-\delta}{2}e^{-j\theta}\Big)=\sin\theta-j\delta\cos\theta,
  \qquad \theta=\frac{\pi V}{2V_\pi},\quad \delta=10^{-\mathrm{ER}/20},
\end{equation}
so $|E_{\mathrm{out}}|^2=\sin^2\theta+\delta^2\cos^2\theta$ swings between $\delta^2$ and 1, and the imbalance leaves a
small residual chirp. With \prm{drive}\code{=single_arm} ($V_1=V$, $V_2=0$) the output becomes
$e^{j\theta}\sin\theta$: the phase follows the drive, the chirp of a single-drive modulator. The metrics
are the average output power and the modulated extinction ratio.
\codeexcerpt{mzm_field}{Field transfer of the MZM with arm imbalance and drive mode.}
\codeexcerpt{mzm_block}{CW laser and MZM (\blk{mach_zehnder_modulator}).}

\blk{intensity_modulator} is the ideal transmitter assumed by the IM/DD formulas of
\cref{sec:imdd}: the drive is mapped linearly to the power,
\begin{equation}
  P(t)=\bar P\big(1+m\,x(t)\big),\qquad m=\frac{\mathrm{ER}-1}{\mathrm{ER}+1},
\end{equation}
so a PAM-M drive with equally spaced levels gives powers equally spaced between $P_1$ and
$P_M=\mathrm{ER}\,P_1$, with no chirp. It stands for a well-linearized external modulator or EML.
\codeexcerpt{im_mod}{Linear intensity modulation (\blk{intensity_modulator}).}

\subsection{The IQ modulator and the polarization-multiplexed transmitter}\label{sec:iq}
Two MZMs biased at null, nested in a larger interferometer with a $90^\circ$ phase shift on one
branch, modulate the in-phase and quadrature components of one polarization:
\begin{equation}
  E_x(t)=E_I(t)+jE_Q(t)\propto\sin\Big(\frac\pi2\frac{v_I(t)}{V_\pi}\Big)+j\sin\Big(\frac\pi2\frac{v_Q(t)}{V_\pi}\Big).
\end{equation}
A polarization-multiplexed (PM-QAM) transmitter splits one laser into two IQ modulators and
combines their outputs on orthogonal polarizations with a polarization beam combiner (PBS),
$\vect E(t)=E_x(t)\hat x+E_y(t)\hat y$. The transmitter DSP generates the four drive signals
$v_{Ix},v_{Qx},v_{Iy},v_{Qy}$ from pulse-shaped constellation points, often pre-distorted for the
sine law, and the drivers amplify them to the $V_\pi$ range. Small swings are nearly linear; large
swings compress the outer constellation points (\cref{fig:mzm}).

\paragraph{Signal normalization.} The four components are pulse trains,
$E_{Ix}(t)=\sum_na_{Ix,n}g(t-nT)$ and so on, combined into the vector symbols
$\vect a_n=a_{x,n}\hat x+a_{y,n}\hat y$ with $a_{x,n}=a_{Ix,n}+ja_{Qx,n}$. With
$\E\{|\vect a_n|^2\}=\E|a_{x,n}|^2+\E|a_{y,n}|^2=1$ and a unit-energy pulse, $\int|g|^2dt=1$,
\begin{equation}
  \vect E(t)=\sqrt{\frac{P_{\mathrm{sig}}}{R_s}}\sum_n\vect a_ng(t-nT)=\sqrt{E_s}\sum_n\vect a_ng(t-nT),\qquad
  \langle|\vect E(t)|^2\rangle=P_{\mathrm{sig}},
\end{equation}
with $E_s=P_{\mathrm{sig}}T$ the energy per (four-dimensional) symbol. For PM-QPSK with power split equally,
$\E|a_{x,n}|^2=\frac12$ and the points are $\frac12(\pm1\pm j)$ on each polarization.

\blk{iq_modulator} implements this transmitter: the complex drive is either mapped linearly to
the field (\prm{model}\code{=linear}) or passed through the two sine laws with peak swing
\prm{drive_swing}$\times V_\pi$, a finite child-MZM extinction ratio and an error of the $90^\circ$
phase (\prm{quadrature_error_deg}). The output is scaled to the launch power \prm{power_dbm}, as a
booster amplifier would, with the power split equally between polarizations, and the laser phase
noise of \prm{linewidth_hz} is applied to both.

\section{Photodetection}\label{sec:photodetection}
\subsection{PIN photodiodes}
A PIN photodiode (p-doped, intrinsic, n-doped) converts photons into conduction electrons by
absorption, ideally one electron per photon, so the current is proportional to the power:
\begin{equation}
  i(t)=\mathcal R\,P(t).
\end{equation}
In CW the photon rate is $P/hf$ and the electron rate $\eta_{ph}P/hf$, with the quantum efficiency
$\eta_{ph}\le1$, so the responsivity is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal R=\frac{q}{hf}\eta_{ph}\quad[\mathrm{A/W}].
\end{equation}
$q/hf$ is 1.25\,A/W at 1550\,nm, 1.07\,A/W at 1330\,nm and 0.68\,A/W at 850\,nm, so $\mathcal R\approx1$, 0.9 and
0.6\,A/W for typical efficiencies (the value close to 1\,A/W in the C band is a numerical
coincidence). The responsivity grows with wavelength up to the bandgap cutoff of the material (Si,
Ge, InGaAs) and then drops. \blk{photodiode} takes \prm{responsivity} or computes it from
\prm{quantum_efficiency} and the signal wavelength.

The currents are small. A +1\,dBm transmitter, 80\,km of fiber at 0.22\,dB/km and 2\,dB of
connectors give $-18.6$\,dBm (14\,\textmu W) and, with $\mathcal R=0.9$\,A/W, 12.4\,\textmu A: an electrical amplifier
is indispensable.

\subsection{The transimpedance amplifier and its noise}
A direct-detection receiver is a photodiode followed by a transimpedance amplifier (TIA),
$V_{TIA}(t)=G_{TIA}\,i(t)$ with $G_{TIA}$ in ohms (1600--2700\,\textohm{} for a 10GbE TIA, giving 25\,mV
from 12.4\,\textmu A), a low-pass filter, sampling at $t_i=t_s+iT_s$ and the decision. The TIA noise is well
described by an additive Gaussian current $n_I(t)$ at its input. Datasheets give it as
\begin{itemize}
  \item the input-referred RMS current $\sigma_I$ over the amplifier bandwidth, or its variance $\sigma_I^2$;
  \item the one-sided input-referred noise density (IRND) in A/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$, the square root of
  the noise PSD,
\end{itemize}
related through the equivalent noise bandwidth $B_{eq}$ of the receiver:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_I=\mathrm{IRND}\sqrt{B_{eq}},\qquad \sigma_I^2=\mathrm{IRND}^2B_{eq}.
\end{equation}
Examples: 1100\,nA$_{\mathrm{rms}}$ over 6\,GHz is 14\,pA/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$; 1.4\,\textmu A over 21\,GHz is
9.7\,pA/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$; research TIAs above 35\,GHz have 12--25\,pA/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. With a CW signal and TIA noise
only, the electrical SNR is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}_e=\frac{i_{Rx}^2}{\sigma_{TIA}^2}=\frac{(\mathcal RP_{Rx})^2}{\mathrm{IRND}^2B_{eq}} .
\end{equation}
For the example above ($i_{Rx}=12$\,\textmu A, $\sigma_I=1.1$\,\textmu A) it is 20.8\,dB: signal and noise are
comparable. The TIA gain does not change the SNR (it multiplies both), and the bandwidth cannot be
reduced below what the baud rate needs. Lower received powers need better receivers.

\subsection{Quantum noise and shot noise}
Even with a noiseless amplifier, the quantum nature of light limits detection. The number of
photons arriving in a window $\Delta T$ is a Poisson variable with mean and variance
\begin{equation}
  \lambda=\E\{N_{Rx}\}=\Var\{N_{Rx}\}=\frac{P_{Rx}\Delta T}{hf_0},
\end{equation}
nearly Gaussian above about 10. The quantum-limited SNR is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}_Q=\frac{\E\{N\}^2}{\Var\{N\}}=\frac{P_{Rx}\Delta T}{hf_0},
\end{equation}
an absolute bound for any photodetection. At 10\,Gb/s ($\Delta T=100$\,ps) and $-20$\,dBm it is 39.9\,dB,
against 17\,dB for a PIN + TIA receiver with 20\,pA/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ over 5\,GHz; the quantum limit would
reach 17\,dB at $-41.9$\,dBm. In practice the TIA noise dominates by more than 20\,dB.

The same discreteness appears in the current as \emph{shot noise}: $I=q\xi$ with $\xi$ the Poisson
electron rate gives the one-sided PSD $2qI$ and, over $B_{eq}$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{shot}}^2=2qB_{eq}\mathcal RP$. With
$B_{eq}=1/(2\Delta T)$ and $\eta_{ph}=1$, $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{shot}}=\mathrm{SNR}_Q$. Both sources together give
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{PIN}}=\frac{(\mathcal RP_{Rx})^2}{\mathrm{IRND}^2B_{eq}+2qB_{eq}\mathcal RP_{Rx}} .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Avalanche photodiodes}
An avalanche photodiode (APD) multiplies each photoelectron by $M$ (10--30) in a high-field
region, so $\mathcal R_{APD}=M\mathcal R$ and $i=M\mathcal RP$. The multiplication is itself random: the shot noise is
amplified by $M^2$ and increased by the excess noise factor $F(M)>1$,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{APD}^2=2qB_{eq}M^2F\mathcal RP_{Rx},\qquad
  \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{APD}}=\frac{(\mathcal RMP_{Rx})^2}{\mathrm{IRND}^2B_{eq}+2qB_{eq}M^2F\mathcal RP_{Rx}} .
\end{equation}
McIntyre's theory gives $F=kM+(1-k)(2-1/M)$ with the ionization ratio $k$; datasheets often give the
power law $F=M^x$ (InGaAs: $x\approx0.7$). The gain raises the signal above the TIA noise but the SNR
grows less than $M^2$; for each power there is an optimum $M$. APDs are better than PINs at low
power (\cref{fig:snr-receivers}) but more expensive, need a high reverse bias and have less
bandwidth (avalanche build-up time). Single-photon avalanche detectors (SPADs), used in quantum
communications, operate far beyond breakdown and fire on single photons.

\subsection{The photodiode block}
\blk{photodiode} sums the powers of both polarizations, multiplies by $M\mathcal R$ and adds Gaussian
noise generated over the simulated band $f_s/2$: TIA noise $\mathrm{IRND}^2f_s/2$, shot noise
$2qM^2F(\mathcal RP+I_d)f_s/2$ with the dark current $I_d$, and the APD excess noise (McIntyre or power
law). The receiver bandwidth then sets $B_{eq}$: with \prm{bandwidth_ghz} a fourth-order
Bessel--Thomson response (the reference receiver of optical Ethernet) filters signal and noise,
and the reported \code{noise_var} is scaled by its noise bandwidth $\frac1{f_s}\int|H|^2df$, so the result
does not depend on the sample rate. With \prm{transimpedance_ohm} the output is the TIA voltage.

\section{IM/DD system performance}\label{sec:imdd}
\subsection{PAM-M with signal-independent noise}
IM/DD systems carry the information in $M$ power levels $P_1<\dots<P_M$, equally spaced by
$\Delta P$, with extinction ratio $\mathrm{ER}=P_M/P_1$. Then
\begin{equation}
  P_1=\frac{(M-1)\Delta P}{\mathrm{ER}-1},\qquad P_M=\frac{\mathrm{ER}\,(M-1)\Delta P}{\mathrm{ER}-1},\qquad
  \bar P=\frac{\mathrm{ER}+1}{2(\mathrm{ER}-1)}(M-1)\Delta P,
\end{equation}
so for a given average power the level spacing is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta P=\frac{\mathrm{ER}-1}{\mathrm{ER}+1}\,\frac{2\bar P}{M-1}.
\end{equation}
The sampled current is $i_k=\mathcal RP_k+n$ with Gaussian TIA noise of variance $\sigma^2=\mathrm{IRND}^2B_{eq}$,
the same on every level (shot noise neglected, as for PIN receivers). With thresholds half-way
between levels, every symbol has one or two neighbours at distance $\Delta I/2=\mathcal R\Delta P/2$, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SER}=\frac{M-1}{M}\erfc\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta I^2}{8\sigma^2}}\Big)
  =\frac{M-1}{M}\erfc\Big(\frac{\mathcal R\bar P}{M-1}\,\frac{\mathrm{ER}-1}{\mathrm{ER}+1}\,\frac{1}{\mathrm{IRND}\sqrt{2B_{eq}}}\Big),
  \label{eq:pam-ser}
\end{equation}
and with Gray labels $\mathrm{BER}\approx\mathrm{SER}/\log_2M$. For PAM-2 and PAM-4,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{PAM2}}=\frac12\erfc(\gamma\bar P),\qquad \mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{PAM4}}\approx\frac38\erfc\Big(\frac{\gamma\bar P}{3}\Big),\qquad
  \gamma=\frac{\mathcal R}{\mathrm{IRND}}\,\frac{\mathrm{ER}-1}{\mathrm{ER}+1}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2B_{eq}}} .
\end{equation}
Below $3\times10^{-2}$ the tail $\erfc(y)\approx e^{-y^2}/(\sqrt\pi y)$ shows that the BER drops by decades within a few
dB. \Cref{fig:pam-imdd} compares the chain \blk{intensity_modulator}$\to$\blk{photodiode}$\to$\blk{agc}$\to$\blk{demapper}
with \cref{eq:pam-ser}. The \emph{sensitivity} is the average power at the target BER.

\subsection{Penalties and sensitivity}\label{sec:imdd-penalties}
\Cref{eq:pam-ser} gives the power penalties of IM/DD directly.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PAM-M at the same baud rate}: the argument scales as $1/(M-1)$, so PAM-M needs
  $10\log_{10}(M-1)$ more average power than PAM-2 (4.8\,dB for PAM-4).
  \item \textbf{PAM-M at the same bit rate}: the baud rate and $B_{eq}$ drop by $n_{\mathrm{bit}}=\log_2M$, so the
  penalty is $10\log_{10}\big((M-1)/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{bit}}}\big)$, 3.3\,dB for PAM-4. Unlike coherent formats,
  multilevel IM/DD always costs power.
  \item \textbf{Finite extinction ratio}: $10\log_{10}\frac{\mathrm{ER}+1}{\mathrm{ER}-1}$ relative to an infinite ER: 0.9\,dB
  at 10\,dB, 1.4\,dB at 8\,dB, 3.7\,dB at 4\,dB. External modulators reach 10--14\,dB, DMLs less than 8\,dB.
  \item \textbf{Bandwidth}: $P_{\mathrm{sens}}\propto\sqrt{B_{eq}}$ in theory, between $B^{0.5}$ and $B^{1.5}$ in practice.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The $\gamma$ approach.} Since the BER depends on $\gamma\bar P$ only, a receiver datasheet that
quotes the sensitivity $P_{\mathrm{sens}}$ at a target BER fixes $\gamma=\eta/P_{\mathrm{sens}}$ with
$\eta=\erfc^{-1}(2\,\mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{sens}})$ (PAM-2), from which the BER at any power or for PAM-4 follows. Values
of $\eta$: 2.19 at $10^{-3}$, 3.02 at $10^{-5}$, 4.24 at $10^{-9}$, 4.97 at $10^{-12}$, 5.62 at $10^{-15}$. For example a
2.5\,Gb/s PIN receiver with $-22$\,dBm sensitivity at $10^{-9}$ has $\gamma=4.24/6.3\,\mathrm{\mu W}=672$\,mW$^{-1}$; a 10\,Gb/s
APD receiver at $-26$\,dBm has 1688\,mW$^{-1}$. The approach is excellent for PIN + TIA receivers, crude
for APDs (signal-dependent noise) and wrong for optically pre-amplified receivers, where ASE
beat noise dominates.

\subsection{Bandwidth limitations and equalization}
When the end-to-end bandwidth falls below about half the bit rate, inter-symbol interference
closes the eye. With a fourth-order Bessel receiver, $B_{3\mathrm{dB}}=0.9R_B$ is practically perfect,
$0.6R_B$ (the SONET reference filter) is good, $0.3R_B$ halves the eye opening (3\,dB penalty).
Commercial optoelectronic bandwidths are 20--25\,GHz (40--50\,GHz in research), so 50--100\,GBd
links need adaptive equalization after the TIA: a feed-forward FIR filter (\blk{lms_equalizer},
\cref{ch:fading}) reduces the ISI at the cost of noise enhancement, and a decision-feedback
equalizer (\blk{dfe_equalizer}) cancels post-cursors without it.

\subsection{Power budget}
The power available for losses is $L_{\mathrm{avail}}=P_{TX,\mathrm{dBm}}-P_{\mathrm{sens,dBm}}$. It must cover the fiber, the
splices (0.05--0.2\,dB each), the connectors (0.1--0.5\,dB), bends, the propagation penalties of
dispersion and nonlinearity, and a system margin of 1--6\,dB for ageing and repairs:
\begin{equation}
  P_{TX}-P_{\mathrm{sens}}\ge\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}L+\sum A_{\mathrm{splice}}+\sum A_{\mathrm{conn}}+A_{\mathrm{penalties}}+\mu .
\end{equation}
For example a 2.5\,Gb/s link with 0\,dBm, $-22$\,dBm sensitivity, 0.25\,dB/km, a 0.1\,dB splice every
2\,km, four 0.4\,dB connectors, 2\,dB of bends, 0.25\,dB of penalties and 6\,dB of margin reaches
40.5\,km. Passive components enter the budget too: a $1\times N$ splitter divides the power by $N$
times an excess loss, $A_{\mathrm{dB}}=10\log_{10}N+\epsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}$ (passive optical networks work around
$-25$\,dBm); a 100\,GHz WDM demultiplexer has up to 2.5\,dB insertion loss, 26\,dB adjacent and 35\,dB
non-adjacent isolation. \blk{optical_attenuator} models a variable attenuator, a splitter
(\prm{split_ways}, \prm{excess_loss_db}) or a connector loss; \blk{fiber} has
\prm{connector_loss_db} at its input.

\subsection{FEC in IM/DD}
A Reed--Solomon RS(255,239) code (7\,\% overhead) turns a pre-FEC BER of $10^{-4}$ into about $10^{-14}$;
modern hard- and soft-decision FECs accept $3.8\times10^{-3}$ to $2\times10^{-2}$. Because the electrical SNR of
IM/DD is proportional to the \emph{square} of the optical power, the optical coding gain is
$10\log_{10}(\eta_{\mathrm{noFEC}}/\eta_{\mathrm{FEC}})$, half the electrical one: from $10^{-15}$ to $3.8\times10^{-3}$ it is
4.7\,dB optical, while an AWGN channel would gain $20\log_{10}$ of the same ratio, 9.5\,dB.

\subsection{The quantum limit of OOK}
An ideal photon counter decides "1" on the arrival of at least one photon. With $P_0=0$ errors
happen only when a "1", carrying on average $N_1=2\bar P/(hfR_B)$ photons, delivers none:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BER}=\frac12e^{-N_1}=\frac12\exp\Big(-\frac{2\bar P}{hfR_B}\Big).
\end{equation}
$10^{-15}$ needs $N_1=34$, about 17 photons per bit on average; $10^{-2}$ only two. Real receivers are
20--30\,dB away, except SPAD receivers at very low rates.

\subsection{Chromatic dispersion in IM/DD}\label{sec:imdd-cd}
Dispersion acts linearly on the field, $E_{Rx}(f)=E_{Tx}(f)H_F(f)$, but the photodiode detects
$|E_{Rx}(t)|^2$: the end-to-end IM/DD channel is \emph{nonlinear} and cannot be equalized exactly, unlike
the coherent channel where the DSP inverts $H_F$. The course uses a heuristic criterion for
chirp-free PAM-2: the eye is acceptable while the differential group delay within the signal
spectrum stays below half a bit slot,
\begin{equation}
  |\Delta\tau_g|=|D|L\Delta\lambda\le\frac{T_B}{2}=\frac{1}{2R_B},\qquad \Delta\lambda=\frac{\lambda^2}{c}\Delta f,\qquad
  \Delta f\approx\frac23R_B,
\end{equation}
the last value found by simulations and experiments for NRZ. At 1550\,nm this gives the
\emph{dispersion limit}
\begin{equation}
  L_{\max}=\frac{1}{10\,|D|\,R_B^2}=\frac{1}{8\,|\beta_2|\,R_B^2}
  \qquad(R_B\ \text{in Tb/s},\ D\ \text{in ps/(nm km)},\ \beta_2\ \text{in ps}^2/\text{km},\ L\ \text{in km}).
  \label{eq:cd-limit}
\end{equation}
For standard fiber ($D=16$) it is 1000\,km at 2.5\,Gb/s and 62.5\,km at 10\,Gb/s. $L_{\max}\propto1/R_B^2$:
multiplying the bit rate by $N$ divides the reach by $N^2$ (16 for SDH steps, 100 for Ethernet
steps). For PAM-M the bandwidth and the symbol slot follow the baud rate $R_B/n_{\mathrm{bit}}$, hence
\begin{equation}
  L_{\max}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{bit}}^2}{10|D|R_B^2},
\end{equation}
four times more for PAM-4 than PAM-2 at the same bit rate. With several fiber types only the
accumulated dispersion counts, $|D_{\mathrm{acc}}|=|\sum D_mL_m|\le1/(10R_B^2)$ (1000\,ps/nm at 10\,Gb/s), which
dispersion-compensating fiber can restore. The other remedies are higher-order PAM and operation
near the zero-dispersion wavelength (O band).

\Cref{fig:cd-limit} tests the criterion with noise-free simulations of \blk{intensity_modulator}
and \blk{fiber}: the eye-opening penalty of 10 and 25\,Gb/s NRZ falls on one curve when the
length is normalized to \cref{eq:cd-limit}, confirming the $R_B^2$ scaling, and is about 0.9\,dB at
$L_{\max}$. PAM-4 at its own $L_{\max}$ already has a 4\,dB penalty: its eyes are a third as high, so the
$n_{\mathrm{bit}}^2$ rule is optimistic, and a 1\,dB penalty is reached at about half that distance. As the
course stresses, such formulas are indicative; the joint effect of power budget and dispersion
needs simulations such as the \emph{10G OOK with a Mach--Zehnder modulator} scenario. With chirp
(\cref{sec:chirp}) the reach is much shorter.

\section{Short-reach links: VCSELs and multimode fiber}\label{sec:mmf}
Inside a data center most links are shorter than a few hundred metres. They use 850\,nm VCSELs
over graded-index multimode fiber (MMF). The 50\,\textmu m core makes coupling and connectors cheap, but
it guides hundreds of modes. 100GBASE-SR4, for example, sends four lanes of 25.78\,GBd NRZ over
70\,m of OM3 or 100\,m of OM4; the reach is set by dispersion, not loss. This section builds the
fiber model from the mode theory of graded-index cores~\cite{gloge1973,olshansky1976} and links it
to the bandwidth model of the IEEE~802.3 link model~\cite{cunningham2002}. The block is
\blk{multimode_fiber}, in \file{phy/multimode.py}; the transmitter is the \blk{vcsel} of
\cref{sec:vcsel}.

\subsection{Why the fiber acts on power}
Write the field in the fiber as a sum of guided modes, $E(\mathbf r,t)=\sum_ma_m(t)\psi_m(\mathbf r)$,
with orthonormal profiles, $\int\psi_m\psi_n^*\,dA=\delta_{mn}$. A photodiode that collects the whole core
measures $P_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=\int|E|^2dA=\sum_m|a_m(t)|^2$: the cross terms vanish by orthogonality. Each mode
carries a fraction $w_m$ of the launched power and arrives after its own group delay $\tau_m$, so
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=10^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}L/10}\sum_mw_m\,P_{\mathrm{in}}(t-\tau_m)
  =10^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}L/10}\,(h*P_{\mathrm{in}})(t).
  \label{eq:mmf-power}
\end{equation}
The fiber is a linear system for the optical \emph{power}, not for the field. Chromatic dispersion
fits the same picture. A multimode VCSEL has a spectrum about 0.4\,nm wide (RMS, roughly 170\,GHz),
far wider than the modulation, so its spectral components add incoherently. A component at
wavelength $\lambda$ is delayed by $DL(\lambda-\lambda_c)$, so the intensity response is the source spectrum
mapped onto delays: for a Gaussian spectrum of RMS width $\sigma_\lambda$, a Gaussian of RMS width
$|D|L\sigma_\lambda$. The total response is the modal response convolved with the chromatic one,
$H(f)=H_{\mathrm{modal}}(f)H_{\mathrm{cd}}(f)$, and the variances add.

\begin{keypoint}
SMF links propagate a coherent field and dispersion is an all-pass filter on the field. VCSEL--MMF
links propagate incoherent power and dispersion is a low-pass filter on the intensity.
\blk{multimode_fiber} therefore filters $|E|^2$ and rebuilds the field as $\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{out}}}$ with the
input phase. It is meant for direct detection.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Mode groups of an $\alpha$-profile core}
Graded-index cores follow a power law, $n^2(r)=n_1^2\big[1-2\Delta(r/a)^\alpha\big]$ for $r<a$, with core
radius $a$, peak index $n_1$ and contrast $\Delta=\mathrm{NA}^2/(2n_1^2)$ ($\Delta=\mmfdelta$ for the standard 50\,\textmu m,
NA\,=\,0.2 fiber). In the WKB approximation~\cite{gloge1973} the number of modes with propagation
constant above $\beta$ is
\begin{equation}
  m(\beta)=M\Big[\frac{n_1^2k^2-\beta^2}{2\Delta n_1^2k^2}\Big]^{\frac{\alpha+2}{\alpha}},\qquad
  M=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2}\,a^2k^2n_1^2\Delta=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2}\,\frac{V^2}{2},
\end{equation}
with $V=ka\,\mathrm{NA}$. Inverting it gives $\beta=n_1k\sqrt{1-2\Delta x}$ with $x=(m/M)^{\alpha/(\alpha+2)}$.

\paragraph{Group delay.} The delay per unit length is $\tau/L=c^{-1}d\beta/dk$. Keeping $m$ fixed and
noting $M\propto k^2n_1^2$, $dx/dk=-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2}x\frac{2N_1}{n_1k}$ with group index $N_1=d(n_1k)/dk$, and
\begin{equation}
  \tau=\frac{LN_1}{c}\;\frac{1-\frac{4\Delta x}{\alpha+2}}{\sqrt{1-2\Delta x}} .
  \label{eq:mmf-exact}
\end{equation}
With $\Delta\approx0.01$ a second-order expansion loses nothing. Including the profile dispersion
$\epsilon=-\frac{2n_1}{N_1}\frac{\lambda}{\Delta}\frac{d\Delta}{d\lambda}$ gives the Olshansky--Keck
form~\cite{olshansky1976}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\tau}{LN_1/c}-1=\Delta C_1x+\Delta^2C_2x^2,\qquad
  C_1=\frac{\alpha-2-\epsilon}{\alpha+2},\quad C_2=\frac{3\alpha-2-2\epsilon}{2(\alpha+2)} .
  \label{eq:mmf-delay}
\end{equation}
With $\epsilon=0$ it is the Taylor expansion of \cref{eq:mmf-exact}; the test suite checks the agreement
to $\mathcal O(\Delta^3)$.

\paragraph{Overfilled RMS width and the optimum profile.} If every mode carries the same power,
$u=m/M$ is uniform and $x=u^q$, $q=\alpha/(\alpha+2)$, has moments $\E[x^k]=1/(kq+1)$, so
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\sigma_\tau}{LN_1/c}=\Delta\Big[C_1^2\Var x+2\Delta C_1C_2\operatorname{Cov}(x,x^2)+\Delta^2C_2^2\Var x^2\Big]^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
The leading term vanishes for $\alpha=2+\epsilon$; minimizing the whole expression gives~\cite{olshansky1976}
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\mathrm{opt}}=2+\epsilon-\Delta\frac{(4+\epsilon)(3+\epsilon)}{5+2\epsilon},\qquad
  \sigma_{\min}\approx\frac{LN_1\Delta^2}{20\sqrt3\,c},
\end{equation}
$\alpha_{\mathrm{opt}}=\mmfalphaopt$ and $\sigma_{\min}=\mmfsigmamin$\,ps/km for this fiber. A parabolic core ($\alpha=2$) is five
times worse, and a profile error of $\pm0.01$ multiplies the spread by two or three
(\cref{fig:mmf-profile}): real fibers are therefore graded by measured bandwidth.

\paragraph{Discrete groups.} Modes with equal principal mode number are degenerate. A core holds
about $G=\sqrt M$ groups (\mmfgroups{} for OM4 at 850\,nm); group $\mu$ holds the $2\mu-1$ modes between
$u=((\mu-1)/G)^2$ and $(\mu/G)^2$. The block evaluates \cref{eq:mmf-delay} at the group centre and
removes the mean delay.

\subsection{Launch conditions}
The power distribution $w_\mu$ depends on the launch.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Overfilled launch} (every mode equally): $w_\mu\propto2\mu-1$, the definition of the
  overfilled-launch (OFL) bandwidth.
  \item \textbf{Gaussian beam} (\prm{launch}\code{=gaussian}). In a parabolic core the modes are the
  Hermite--Gauss modes of a two-dimensional harmonic oscillator with fundamental field radius
  $w_0=\sqrt{a\lambda/(\pi\,\mathrm{NA})}\approx5.8$\,\textmu m; group $p=\mu-1$ collects the modes with $n_x+n_y=p$, and a
  beam of radius $w$ displaced by $r_0$ separates into $x$ and $y$ overlap integrals.
\end{itemize}
Two exact cases check the computation (\cref{fig:mmf-launch}): a matched beam displaced by $r_0$ is a
coherent state, with Poisson group powers of mean $r_0^2/w_0^2$; a centred beam of the wrong size is a
squeezed state, which excites only even groups with weights $(1-t)t^{p/2}$,
$t=\big((w^2-w_0^2)/(w^2+w_0^2)\big)^2$. A centred launch puts the power in the low groups, which travel
close together: this is why the effective modal bandwidth (EMB) of VCSEL links exceeds the OFL
bandwidth (\cref{tab:om}).

\paragraph{Gaussian bandwidth model} (\prm{model}\code{=bandwidth}, the default). The IEEE~802.3 link
model assumes Gaussian responses: for $H(f)=\exp(-2\pi^2\sigma^2f^2)$, $H(B)=\frac12$ gives

\subsection{A 25G data-center lane}
The \emph{25G VCSEL multimode data-center link} scenario chains \blk{pulse_shaper} (NRZ),
\blk{vcsel} (7\,mA bias, $\pm3$\,mA), a 6\,dB coupling loss, \blk{multimode_fiber}, \blk{photodiode} with
the reference receiver (0.75$R_s$ Bessel--Thomson) and \blk{agc}. \Cref{fig:mmf-eyes} shows the

\begin{pitfall}[What the multimode model leaves out]
The IEEE link model also budgets mode-partition noise (the VCSEL modes fluctuate in power while
the total stays constant, which dispersion turns into noise) and modal noise (speckle filtered by
connector offsets); neither is modelled. The mode-group model ignores the coupling between modal
and chromatic dispersion (each group has its own wavelength dependence~\cite{golowich2003}),
differential mode attenuation and mode coupling. Statistically realistic fibers would need DMD
templates measured according to IEC~60793-1-49.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Optical amplifiers}\label{sec:amplifiers}
Optical amplifiers amplify the light directly, without conversion to the electrical domain,
and are fiber-pigtailed so they can be inserted anywhere in a link. Commercial since about
1990, they made multi-span links of thousands of kilometres possible. Three types are used:
erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs), semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs) and Raman
amplifiers (\cref{sec:raman}).

\subsection{Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers}
The core of a 5--30\,m fiber is doped with Er$^{3+}$ ions (100--1000\,ppm), a quasi three-level system.
A pump laser at 980\,nm lifts electrons from the ground level $E_1$ to the pump band $E_3$, from
which they decay in about 1\,\textmu s to the metastable level $E_2$; its long lifetime
($\tau_{21}\approx8$--12\,ms) lets the population of $E_2$ build up until it is inverted with respect to
$E_1$. The $E_2\to E_1$ transition matches 1530--1570\,nm, so C-band signal photons are amplified by
stimulated emission. Pumping at 1480\,nm excites the top of the $E_2$ manifold directly. The levels
are broadened by the glass host (Stark splitting), so the gain covers the whole C band (and, with
longer fibers, the L band), with a peak near 1530\,nm. A basic EDFA has an isolator, a WDM coupler
that adds the pump, the doped fiber, a coupler that removes the residual pump and an output
isolator; commercial units add several stages, counter-propagating pumps, gain-flattening filters
(GFF), power monitors, a spectrum analyzer and a control CPU.

\subsection{Semiconductor optical amplifiers}
An SOA is a laser chip without feedback: the current injected into a forward-biased junction
creates the inversion. It is cheap, small, integrable and can cover the O to L bands, but has a
higher noise figure (7--8\,dB), polarization dependence and fast carrier dynamics (ns), which cause
cross-gain modulation and four-wave mixing between WDM channels. SOAs are used as single-channel
boosters and preamplifiers and for switching and wavelength conversion.

\subsection{System model and ASE noise}
On the field, an amplifier of power gain $G$ is linear (below saturation) and adds noise:
\begin{equation}
  \vect E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=\sqrt G\,\vect E_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+\vect n(t).
\end{equation}
The noise is amplified spontaneous emission (ASE), emitted on both polarizations. After gain
flattening it is white and Gaussian on all four quadratures,
$\vect n=(n_{Rx}+jn_{Ix})\hat x+(n_{Ry}+jn_{Iy})\hat y$, each with power spectral density $N_0/2$,
\begin{equation}
  N_0=hf\,(G-1)\,n_{sp},
\end{equation}
where $n_{sp}\ge1$ is the spontaneous emission (inversion) factor. System specifications use the noise
figure $F=2n_{sp}$, so the quantum limit is $F\ge2$ (3\,dB). Very good C-band EDFAs have 4.5--5\,dB
($n_{sp}=1.4$--1.6), cheaper ones 5--6\,dB, L-band EDFAs about 1\,dB more, SOAs 7--8\,dB. The ASE power in a
bandwidth $B$ (both polarizations, four quadratures) is
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{ASE}}=4\cdot\frac{N_0}{2}B=2N_0B=hf\,(G-1)\,F\,B .
\end{equation}
The density per polarization of the complex envelope is $S_{\mathrm{ASE}}=N_0$; this is the quantity carried
in \meta{ase_psd}. (The exact quantum-optical definition, $F=2n_{sp}(G-1)/G+1/G$, coincides with
$F=2n_{sp}$ at high gain; LinkStudio follows the course definition.)
\codeexcerpt{ase}{ASE density per polarization, $n_{sp}=F/2$.}
\codeexcerpt{add_ase}{ASE as circular Gaussian noise in the simulated bandwidth.}

\subsection{OSNR of one amplifier}
The generalized signal-to-noise ratio GSNR is the ratio of the signal power to all the noise
added by the link (ASE, nonlinear interference); when only ASE counts it is the \emph{optical} SNR,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{OSNR}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{sig}}}{P_{\mathrm{ASE}}}=\frac{G\,P_{\mathrm{in}}}{hf\,B\,(G-1)\,F}\approx\frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}}{hf\,B\,F}\quad(G\gg1).
\end{equation}
At high gain the OSNR does not depend on $G$, only on the input power and the noise figure: the
amplifier behaves like an input-referred noise source $P_{\mathrm{ASE,in}}\approx hfFB$, the \emph{noise floor}, about
$-58$\,dBm in 0.1\,nm (12.5\,GHz) or $-48.9$\,dBm in 100\,GHz, plus $F_{\mathrm{dB}}$. Hence the "58\,dB rule"
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{OSNR}_{0.1\,\mathrm{nm,dB}}\approx58+P_{\mathrm{in,dBm}}-F_{\mathrm{dB}} .
  \label{eq:osnr58}
\end{equation}
To keep a high OSNR the signal must not fall too close to the floor before it is amplified. The
simulator reports the OSNR both in 0.1\,nm (\code{osnr_db}) and in $B=R_s$ (\code{osnr_rs_db}), the
reference bandwidth of coherent systems.

\paragraph{Gain saturation.} The gain of a real amplifier depends on the total input power. For a
homogeneously broadened amplifier with small-signal gain $G_0$ and saturation power $P_{\mathrm{sat}}$,
\begin{equation}
  G=G_0\exp\Big(-\frac{(G-1)P_{\mathrm{in}}}{P_{\mathrm{sat}}}\Big),
\end{equation}
solved numerically when \prm{saturation_power_dbm} is set. EDFAs saturate on the average power
(their ms lifetime ignores the bits); in long chains working at constant total output power, the
accumulated ASE steals gain from the signal, which then decays along the link (the "droop" effect).
\codeexcerpt{amp_sat}{Saturated gain.}

\blk{optical_amplifier} works in \code{gain} mode (fixed or saturated gain) or \code{power} mode (output
power held at \prm{target_power_dbm}) and reports the OSNR and the gain.

\section{Multi-span links and the OSNR}\label{sec:multispan}
Long-haul links are chains of spans, each a stretch of fiber followed by an amplifier. The
analysis below refers to the power of one WDM channel, uses $B=R_s$ as noise bandwidth and counts
only ASE. Let span $i$ have loss $A_i<1$ (fiber and lumped losses) and amplifier gain $G_i$ and noise
figure $F_i$, with $P_{Tx}$ launched into the first span. The ASE of amplifier $i$ is amplified and
attenuated by all the following spans, so at the receiver
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{ASE,tot}}=hfB\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Big[F_i(G_i-1)\prod_{j=i+1}^{M}G_jA_j\Big],\qquad
  P_{Rx}=P_{Tx}\prod_{k=1}^{M}A_kG_k,
\end{equation}
and $\mathrm{OSNR}=P_{Rx}/P_{\mathrm{ASE,tot}}$.

\paragraph{Transparent spans.} If every amplifier compensates exactly the loss of its span,
$G_iA_i=1$, the products are 1, the launch power into every span is $P_{Tx}$ and
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{OSNR}=\frac{P_{Tx}}{hfB\sum_iF_i(G_i-1)} .
\end{equation}
With identical spans,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{OSNR}=\frac{P_{Tx}}{M\,hfBF(G-1)}\approx\frac{A\,P_{Tx}}{M\,hfBF}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{in,EDFA}}}{M\,hfBF}:
  \label{eq:osnr-identical}
\end{equation}
the signal is restored after every span while the ASE accumulates linearly, so the OSNR falls as
$1/M$: $-3$\,dB for every doubling of the number of spans (or of the length at fixed span length).
This mild scaling is what makes transoceanic links possible.

\paragraph{Non-transparent spans.} With identical spans but $GA\ne1$ the power drifts as $(AG)^M$ and
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{ASE}}=hfBF(G-1)\frac{1-(AG)^M}{1-AG},\qquad
  \mathrm{OSNR}=\frac{P_{Tx}}{hfBF(G-1)}\,\frac{(AG)^M(1-AG)}{1-(AG)^M}.
\end{equation}
\blk{optical_link} uses transparent spans by default and a fixed gain with
\prm{amplifier_gain_db}; \cref{fig:osnr-spans} shows both against the formulas.

\paragraph{The course exercise.} A 3000\,km link of 40 spans of 75\,km (0.2\,dB/km, so $A=-15$\,dB and
$G=15$\,dB), EDFAs with $F=5$\,dB, 0\,dBm per channel at 1550\,nm and $B=R_s=64$\,GHz. After the first span
$\mathrm{OSNR}_1=\osnrfirst$\,dB; after 40 spans $\mathrm{OSNR}_{40}=\mathrm{OSNR}_1-10\log_{10}40=\osnrforty$\,dB. A 64\,GBd PM-16QAM
transceiver needs about 14\,dB, so the link works with 1\,dB of margin, and could grow to
$10^{(31-14)/10}\approx50$ spans (3750\,km). Margin can be created with shorter spans, lower-loss fiber,
better amplifiers, more launch power (up to the nonlinear limit of \cref{sec:gn}) or a lower symbol
rate. The \emph{3000\,km PM-16QAM OSNR budget} scenario runs this link end to end.

\begin{equation}

\section{Raman amplification}\label{sec:raman}
Raman amplifiers, commercial since about 2010, use the transmission fiber itself as gain medium,
through stimulated Raman scattering (SRS): a strong pump scatters on the vibrations of the silica
molecules and transfers energy to photons at lower frequency. The gain peaks about 13\,THz below the
pump, with a gain efficiency up to $C_R\approx0.4$\,W$^{-1}$km$^{-1}$ in standard fiber; a pump near 206\,THz
(1455\,nm) amplifies the C band. Pumps of 0.5--1\,W are typical, and several pumps at different
wavelengths add their gain profiles to cover the C and L bands. The pump is usually injected
backwards from the span end (counter-propagating), which averages its power fluctuations and
reduces the noise transferred to the signal.

The gain is distributed: the signal does not fall as low as in a passive span. The difference
between the output power with the pump on and off is the \emph{on/off gain}. The main benefit is on
the noise: the signal reaches the following EDFA well above its noise floor, so the Raman + EDFA
stage has a lower equivalent noise figure than the EDFA alone. Equivalent noise figures of 0\,dB and
below are possible without violating any law: the amplification starts before the signal has
fully attenuated, which shortens the effective loss of the span (distributed amplification has
its own quantum limit).

\paragraph{Model.} With a counter-propagating pump of input power $P_p$ and pump loss $\alpha_p$, the
signal power gain coefficient along the span is
\begin{gather}
  g(z)=C_RP_p\,e^{-\alpha_p(L-z)},\qquad \frac{dP}{dz}=\big(g(z)-\alpha\big)P,\\
  G_R=\exp\big(C_RP_pL_{\mathrm{eff},p}\big),\qquad L_{\mathrm{eff},p}=\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_pL}}{\alpha_p},
\end{gather}
where $G_R$ is the on/off gain. Spontaneous Raman scattering adds noise with density per polarization
\begin{equation}
  S_R=n_{sp}hf\int_0^Lg(z)\,G(z,L)\,dz,\qquad G(z,L)=\exp\int_z^L\big(g(z')-\alpha\big)dz',
\end{equation}
the ASE born at $z$ amplified to the span end, with $n_{sp}=1/(1-e^{-h\Delta\nu/kT})\approx1.14$ at the 13\,THz
shift and room temperature. Without fiber and pump loss it reduces to the lumped
$n_{sp}hf(G_R-1)$, which the test suite checks. In \blk{optical_link} the gain enters the linear
half-steps of the split-step solver (so the higher power in the second half of the span also
raises the Kerr nonlinearity), the Raman ASE is added at the span end, and the EDFA gain is reduced
to keep the span transparent. The span's equivalent noise figure is reported as
\begin{equation}
  F_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{2(S_RG+S_{\mathrm{EDFA}})}{hf\,(G_{\mathrm{span}}-1)},
\end{equation}
the noise figure of a single lumped amplifier that would compensate the passive span loss
$G_{\mathrm{span}}$ with the same total ASE.

\section{Coherent systems}\label{sec:coherent}
\subsection{Throughput and spectral efficiency}
Backbone links carry from a few to more than 100\,Tb/s per fiber over hundreds to more than
10\,000\,km, with wavelength-division multiplexing (one laser per channel) and coherent
transmission. Since traffic grows by 25--30\,\% per year, the goal is to maximize the throughput
\begin{equation}
  \text{Throughput}=\mathrm{SE}\cdot B,
\end{equation}
the product of the spectral efficiency and the optical bandwidth (5\,THz in the C band, 10\,THz in
C+L, 12\,THz and more in extended bands). For a channel of bit rate $R_b^{\mathrm{ch}}$ occupying
$B_{\mathrm{ch}}$, $\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{ch}}=R_b^{\mathrm{ch}}/B_{\mathrm{ch}}$; for $N_{\mathrm{ch}}$ identical channels spaced by guard bands $\delta_f$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{WDM}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{ch}}R_b^{\mathrm{ch}}}{N_{\mathrm{ch}}B_{\mathrm{ch}}+(N_{\mathrm{ch}}-1)\delta_f}\approx\frac{R_b^{\mathrm{ch}}}{B_{\mathrm{ch}}+\delta_f}.
\end{equation}
With $B_{\mathrm{ch}}=(1+\rho)R_s$ (roll-off $\rho$, 0.1--0.2 today) and $R_b^{\mathrm{ch}}=R_s\cdot\mathrm{IR}$, where the information
rate IR is the number of bits per symbol,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{WDM}}\approx\frac{\mathrm{IR}}{(1+\rho)+\delta_f/R_s},
\end{equation}
so raising the symbol rate alone barely helps: the spectral efficiency grows with IR. IM/DD has
IR\,=\,1 (OOK) and poor spectral control. Coherent systems modulate the four quadratures: PM-BPSK has
IR\,=\,2, PM-QPSK 4, PM-8QAM 6, PM-16QAM 8, PM-64QAM 12. At 64\,GBd, PM-16QAM carries 512\,Gb/s gross; net
rates subtract the FEC overhead (15--25\,\%) and framing (OTN, Ethernet). The throughput of a
multi-fiber cable is $N_{\mathrm{fiber}}B\,\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{WDM}}$, with the bracketed factor $1/((1+\rho)+\delta_f/R_s)\approx0.8$--0.9.

Three generations of commercial systems illustrate the trend: 10--32\,GBd PM-QPSK and PM-16QAM on a
50\,GHz grid (100--200\,Gb/s, 10--20\,Tb/s per fiber, from 2010); up to 64\,GBd and multi-format
transceivers adapting from PM-BPSK to PM-64QAM to the link GSNR (from 2017); about 140\,GBd with
probabilistic constellation shaping and up to 1.2\,Tb/s per transceiver (from 2025), with fewer
wavelengths for the same capacity. Pluggable coherent modules (CFP2-DCO, QSFP-DD, OSFP) serve
data-center interconnect and metro.

\subsection{The coherent receiver}
A single photodiode detects $i\propto|\vect E|^2=|E_{Rx}|^2+|E_{Ix}|^2+|E_{Ry}|^2+|E_{Iy}|^2$: phase and polarization are
lost. Coherent detection adds a strong local oscillator (LO) before the photodiode:
\begin{equation}
  i\propto|\vect E+\vect E_{LO}|^2=|\vect E|^2+|\vect E_{LO}|^2+2\Re\{\vect E\cdot\vect E_{LO}^*\}.
\end{equation}
With $\vect E_{LO}=E_{LOx}\hat x$ real and in phase with the carrier,
$i\propto P_{ph}+P_{LO}+2E_{LOx}E_{Rx}(t)$: the beat term is proportional to one quadrature, boosted by the LO
amplitude. \emph{Balanced detection} removes the common terms: an optical hybrid feeds two photodiodes
with $\vect E+E_{LOx}\hat x$ and $\vect E-E_{LOx}\hat x$, and the difference current is
\begin{equation}
  i_{\mathrm{bal}}=i_1-i_2\propto4E_{LOx}E_{Rx}(t).
\end{equation}
An LO shifted by $90^\circ$ ($jE_{LOx}\hat x$) detects $E_{Ix}$, and the $y$ polarization is handled alike. The
coherent front end (\cref{fig:coherent-fe}) splits signal and LO with polarization beam splitters,
mixes each polarization with the LO in a $90^\circ$ hybrid (outputs $E_x\pm E_{LOx}$, $E_x\pm jE_{LOx}$) and
detects the four outputs with balanced photodetectors; integrated coherent receivers contain all of
it. The LO is a free-running laser: with an arbitrary phase $\alpha$ the "I" output is
$E_{LO}[E_{Rx}\cos\alpha+E_{Ix}\sin\alpha]$, a rotation of the quadratures. The fiber also rotates the
polarization, so the received quadratures are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}E'_{Rx}\\E'_{Ix}\\E'_{Ry}\\E'_{Iy}\end{pmatrix}=\mat M(t)\begin{pmatrix}E_{Rx}\\E_{Ix}\\E_{Ry}\\E_{Iy}\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $\mat M(t)$ a time-varying $4\times4$ orthogonal matrix (a $2\times2$ unitary Jones matrix) for a lossless
fiber without polarization-dependent loss. Estimating and inverting it, together with the LO
phase, is the job of the receiver DSP (\cref{sec:coherent-dsp}).

\blk{coherent_receiver} outputs the complex photocurrent per polarization,
\begin{equation}
  i_I+j\,i_Q=\mathcal R\sqrt{P_{LO}}\;E(t)\,e^{-j(2\pi\Delta f\,t+\phi_{LO}(t))}+n(t),
\end{equation}
with LO frequency offset $\Delta f$ and phase noise $\phi_{LO}$, LO shot noise and TIA noise. With dual
polarization a splitter feeds each hybrid with half of the LO. With a strong LO the LO shot noise
$2q\mathcal RP_{LO}B$ dominates the TIA noise; signal and shot noise then scale together, so the split does
not change the shot-noise-limited SNR, only the margin over TIA noise. \prm{hybrid_phase_error_deg}
models a hybrid that is not exactly $90^\circ$ (I/Q skew).

\subsection{Noise, matched filtering and decision variables}
At the receiver input the field is signal plus ASE,
\begin{equation}
  \vect E_{ph}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{P_s}{R_s}}\sum_n\vect a_ng(t-nT)+\vect n(t),
\end{equation}
with the four ASE components white with density $N_0/2$. An optical band-pass filter (a WDM
demultiplexer, much wider than the channel) shapes them as $\frac{N_0}2|H(f)|^2$, essentially flat over the
channel. The front end delivers the four noisy quadratures, and the optimum detector for AWGN is a
matched filter $h(t)=g^*(-t)$ (made causal with a delay $t_d$), implemented in the DSP. For a
root-raised-cosine $g(t)$ the overall pulse $p=g*g^*(-t)$ is raised cosine, $P(f)=|G(f)|^2$, which
satisfies the Nyquist criterion: no ISI at $t=kT$. Sampling gives the decision variables
\begin{equation}
  d_{k,x}=\sqrt{E_{s,x}}\,a_{k,x}+n_{F,x},\qquad d_{k,y}=\sqrt{E_{s,y}}\,a_{k,y}+n_{F,y},
\end{equation}
with $E_{s,x}=E_{s,y}=E_s/2$ for equal power, and four independent Gaussian noise components of variance
$\sigma^2=\frac{N_0}2\int|g|^2=\frac{N_0}2$.

\subsection{BER of PM-QPSK and the constellation SNR}\label{sec:coherent-snr}
For PM-QPSK each quadrature is an independent binary signal of amplitude $\pm\sqrt{E_s/2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{E_s}}2$
in Gaussian noise of variance $N_0/2$, so
\begin{equation}
  P_e=\int_{-\infty}^{-\sqrt{E_s}/2}\frac{e^{-x^2/N_0}}{\sqrt{\pi N_0}}dx=\frac12\erfc\Big(\sqrt{\frac{E_s}{4N_0}}\Big),
\end{equation}
the BER of each of the four Gray-coded bits. The SNR is the same whether measured on one real
component ($\frac{E_s/4}{N_0/2}$), on one polarization ($\frac{E_s/2}{N_0}$) or on the four-dimensional symbol
($\frac{E_s}{2N_0}$):
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}=\frac{E_s}{2N_0},\qquad \mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{PM\text-QPSK}}=\frac12\erfc\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}2}\Big).
\end{equation}
This "constellation SNR" is measurable optically. The ASE power in $B$ over both polarizations is
$4\frac{N_0}2B=2N_0B$, so the GSNR in the reference bandwidth $B=R_s$ is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{GSNR}_{B=R_s}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{sig}}}{2N_0R_s}=\frac{E_sR_s}{2N_0R_s}=\frac{E_s}{2N_0}=\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{constellation}},
\end{equation}
in the absence of transceiver noise. The apparent factor of 2 with the wireless formula
$\frac12\erfc\sqrt{E_s/(2N_0)}$ for QPSK disappears with consistent definitions: there $E_s$ is the
energy per polarization and $N_0$ the density of the complex noise. In LinkStudio the link metric
\code{osnr_rs_db} is this constellation SNR, equal to $E_s/N_0$ per polarization in the convention of
the baseband chapters.

\subsection{BER of PM-QAM}
For AWGN with Gray labels the course gives, in terms of the constellation SNR per polarization:
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{PM\text-BPSK}}=\frac12\erfc\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}},\qquad
  \mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{PM\text-QPSK}}=\frac12\erfc\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}2},\\
  \mathrm{BER}_{M\text-\mathrm{QAM}}\approx\frac{L-1}{L\log_2L}\erfc\sqrt{\frac{3\,\mathrm{SNR}}{2(L^2-1)}}\quad(M=L^2),\\
  \mathrm{BER}_{8\mathrm{QAM}}\approx\frac23\erfc\sqrt{\frac{3}{14}\mathrm{SNR}},\qquad
  \mathrm{BER}_{16\mathrm{QAM}}\approx\frac38\erfc\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{10}},\\
  \mathrm{BER}_{32\mathrm{QAM}}\approx\frac{13}{40}\erfc\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{20}},\quad
  \mathrm{BER}_{64\mathrm{QAM}}\approx\frac{7}{24}\erfc\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{42}},
\end{gather}
accurate between $3\times10^{-2}$ and $10^{-3}$ and below. The general square-QAM expression, written here
with the prefactor and argument that reproduce the specific cases ($L=2,4,8$), is the

\code{theory.ber_pm_qam}. \Cref{fig:coherent-ber} compares them with back-to-back simulations in which

The information rate actually achieved is below the nominal IR and depends on the SNR: 16-QAM
approaches 4\,bit/symbol per polarization only above about 15\,dB, and at 10\,dB carries about 3.2. A
constellation is used near its maximum IR, where a FEC with 15--27\,\% overhead can correct the
pre-FEC BER (up to a few $10^{-2}$ for soft decision). Typical operating SNRs are 5--8\,dB for QPSK,
8--11\,dB for 8-QAM, 11--14\,dB for 16-QAM, 14--17\,dB for 32-QAM and 17--20\,dB for 64-QAM, so higher IR
requires higher SNR. Soft-decision FEC and probabilistic shaping let the IR follow the channel
capacity more finely.

\subsection{Transceiver noise: ISNR and GSNR}
The SNR on the recovered constellation contains link noise and noise generated inside the
transceiver,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{sig}}}{P_{N,\mathrm{link}}+P_{N,\mathrm{int}}},\qquad
  P_{N,\mathrm{link}}=P_{\mathrm{ASE}}+\eta_{\mathrm{NLI}}P_{\mathrm{sig}}^3,\qquad P_{N,\mathrm{int}}=\chi_{N,\mathrm{int}}P_{\mathrm{sig}}.
\end{equation}
The link noise is ASE plus nonlinear interference (\cref{sec:gn}); the internal noise (electrical
amplifiers, DAC and ADC quantization, modulator and other imperfections) scales with the signal.
Back to back the receiver measures the internal SNR, $\mathrm{ISNR}=1/\chi_{N,\mathrm{int}}$, a property of the
transceiver pair (20--21\,dB for state-of-the-art equipment); the link alone is characterized by
$\mathrm{GSNR}=P_{\mathrm{sig}}/P_{N,\mathrm{link}}$, which reduces to the OSNR when only ASE counts. Then
\begin{equation}
  \frac1{\mathrm{SNR}}=\frac1{\mathrm{GSNR}}+\frac1{\mathrm{ISNR}},\qquad \mathrm{SNR}=\frac{\mathrm{GSNR}\cdot\mathrm{ISNR}}{\mathrm{GSNR}+\mathrm{ISNR}} :
\end{equation}
the link dominates when $\mathrm{ISNR}\gg\mathrm{GSNR}$, the transceiver when $\mathrm{GSNR}\gg\mathrm{ISNR}$. The ISNR caps the usable
constellation (around PM-64QAM for long haul today). \prm{internal_snr_db} of \blk{coherent_receiver}
adds Gaussian noise of variance $P_{\mathrm{sig}}/\mathrm{ISNR}$ to the photocurrents; the test suite checks that a
noiseless input then shows exactly that SNR.

\paragraph{Chromatic dispersion compensation.} The accumulated dispersion is known from the
metadata (or given), and its all-pass response \cref{eq:dispersion-operator} is inverted exactly by
multiplying with its conjugate in the frequency domain (\cref{lst:cd_comp}). In modern systems
there is no optical dispersion compensation: uncompensated transmission (UT).

\paragraph{Frequency-offset estimation.} Raising QPSK to the fourth power removes the modulation:
$(s\,e^{j2\pi\Delta ft})^4=s^4e^{j2\pi4\Delta ft}$ with $s^4=-1$ for every QPSK symbol, and roughly constant on
average for square QAM. The spectrum of $r^4$ has a line at $4\Delta f$; the estimate is its peak divided
by 4, refined by zero-padding and computed on a low-pass copy. In dual polarization a rotated
mixture $as_1+bs_2$ has a fourth-power line proportional to $a^4+b^4$, which can vanish, so the
estimator also uses $(x_0+x_1)/\sqrt2$ and $(x_0+jx_1)/\sqrt2$. Compensating the offset \emph{after} the
dispersion compensator leaves the residual $\exp\big(j\frac{\beta_2L}{2}(2\omega\Delta\omega+\Delta\omega^2)\big)$, a bulk delay
$\beta_2L\Delta\omega$ plus a constant phase, which the block removes.
\codeexcerpt{fo_est}{Fourth-power FO estimation over several polarization combinations.}

\paragraph{Adaptive $2\times2$ equalization.} Polarization rotation, PMD and residual dispersion form
a $2\times2$ MIMO channel, inverted by a fractionally spaced butterfly of four FIR filters
(\cref{fig:butterfly}):
\begin{equation}
  y_p[k]=\sum_{q\in\{x,y\}}\vect w_{pq}\T\,\vect u_q[k],\qquad
  \vect w_{pq}\leftarrow\vect w_{pq}+\mu\,e_p[k]\,\vect u_q\conj[k] .
\end{equation}
The error $e_p$ is either the constant-modulus error $y_p(R-|y_p|^2)$ with $R=\E|s|^4/\E|s|^2$ (Godard's
CMA~\cite{godard1980}), blind and insensitive to the carrier phase, or the LMS error $d_p-y_p$, trained
on known symbols and then decision directed, with a larger step during training.

\paragraph{Carrier phase recovery.} Laser phase noise of $2\times100$\,kHz at 32\,GBd is slow but needs
feed-forward tracking. The Viterbi--Viterbi estimator removes the QPSK modulation with the fourth
power, $\hat\phi=\frac14\big(\arg\sum_{|i|\le W}y_{k+i}^4-\pi\big)$; blind phase search (BPS)~\cite{pfau2009} works for any square
QAM: it rotates the symbols by $B$ test phases in $[-\pi/4,\pi/4)$, sums over a window the squared
distances to the nearest points and keeps the best phase. Both are defined modulo $\pi/2$ and are
unwrapped on $4\hat\phi$; the quadrant ambiguity is resolved once per frame with a short block of known
symbols after the equalizer has converged.

\section{Nonlinear interference and the GN model}\label{sec:gn}
\subsection{Gaussianity of dispersed signals}
In uncompensated transmission the accumulated dispersion is enormous: a 100\,GBd signal spreads over
17 symbols after 10\,km and over 1700 after 1000\,km. The four quadratures of the received field are
then sums of many independent symbols and take an approximately zero-mean, uncorrelated Gaussian
distribution, with the power spectral density of the transmitted signal ($|H_F|=1$): the constellation
before DSP looks like a Gaussian cloud (\cref{fig:coherent-const}, left). The Kerr nonlinearity acts on
this noise-like signal. The nonlinear disturbance that the WDM channels generate on each other and on
themselves, the \emph{nonlinear interference} (NLI), is therefore itself approximately additive
Gaussian noise, like ASE, and adds to it:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{GSNR}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{ch}}}{P_{\mathrm{ASE}}+P_{\mathrm{NLI}}} .
\end{equation}
After CD compensation the NLI appears as a cloud around the constellation points, and the BER follows
from the GSNR with the AWGN formulas.

\subsection{The Gaussian-noise model}
The GN model~\cite{poggiolini2012} gives the NLI power in closed form under these assumptions:
identical channels (symbol rate $R_s$, power $P_{\mathrm{ch}}$, compact spectra), equal spacing $\Delta f$, identical
transparent spans of length $L_s$ with loss coefficient $\alpha$, dispersion $\beta_2$ and nonlinearity $\gamma$. For
one span, with $\alpha$ the \emph{field} loss coefficient ($\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}=8.686\,\alpha$),
\begin{equation}
  P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{NLI}}=\eta^{(1)}P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3,\qquad
  \eta^{(1)}=\frac{8}{27}\,\frac{\gamma^2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{\pi|\beta_2|L_{\mathrm{eff},a}R_s^2}\,
  \operatorname{asinh}\Big(\frac{\pi^2}{2}|\beta_2|L_{\mathrm{eff},a}R_s^2N_{\mathrm{ch}}^{2R_s/\Delta f}\Big),
  \label{eq:gn}
\end{equation}
with $L_{\mathrm{eff}}=(1-e^{-2\alpha L_s})/(2\alpha)$ and $L_{\mathrm{eff},a}=1/(2\alpha)$. For long spans $L_{\mathrm{eff}}\to1/(2\alpha)$ and it reduces to the
form of the course,
\begin{equation}
  \eta^{(1)}\approx\frac{4}{27\pi}\,\frac{\gamma^2}{|\beta_2|\alpha R_s^2}\operatorname{asinh}\Big(\frac{\pi^2}{4\alpha}|\beta_2|R_s^2N_{\mathrm{ch}}^{2R_s/\Delta f}\Big)
  \approx\frac{4}{27\pi}\,\frac{\gamma^2}{|\beta_2|\alpha R_s^2}\ln\Big(\frac{\pi^2}{2\alpha}|\beta_2|R_s^2N_{\mathrm{ch}}^{2R_s/\Delta f}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{asinh}x\approx\ln2x$ for $x>3$, always the case in practice. The exact effective length of
\cref{eq:gn} also makes the NLI vanish correctly for short spans (the course uses the factor
$1-e^{-4\alpha L_s}$ for the same purpose). The formula holds for $R_s\ge25$\,GBd, span loss $\ge7$\,dB,
$|\beta_2|>4$\,ps$^2$/km and roll-off below 0.5. Its main features: the NLI grows as $P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3$ and as $\gamma^2$, and
decreases with $|\beta_2|$, because higher dispersion decorrelates the interacting signals faster.
Assuming incoherent accumulation, $M$ identical spans give $P_{\mathrm{NLI,tot}}=M\eta^{(1)}P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3$.
\codeexcerpt{gn_eta}{The GN-model coefficient $\eta$ of one span (\code{theory.gn_eta}).}

\subsection{Optimum launch power and the 3\,dB rule}
For $M$ identical spans with ASE $P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE}}=hfR_sF(G-1)$ per span,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{GSNR}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{ch}}}{M\big(P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE}}+\eta^{(1)}P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3\big)} .
\end{equation}
At low power the ASE dominates and the GSNR grows with $P_{\mathrm{ch}}$; at high power the NLI dominates and it
falls as $1/P_{\mathrm{ch}}^2$. Setting $d\,\mathrm{GSNR}/dP_{\mathrm{ch}}=0$ gives $P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE}}-2\eta^{(1)}P^3_{\mathrm{ch}}=0$:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{ch,opt}}=\sqrt[3]{\frac{P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE}}}{2\eta^{(1)}}},\qquad
  \mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}\cdot M=\frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{2\eta^{(1)}\big[P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE}}\big]^2}} ,
\end{equation}
independent of the number of spans; the same power maximizes the reach for a target GSNR. At the
optimum $P_{\mathrm{NLI}}=\frac12P_{\mathrm{ASE}}$ (span by span and in total): the ASE is 3\,dB above the NLI, and the GSNR
counting only NLI is twice the OSNR. Lumped losses before the fiber $A_{\mathrm{pre}}$ reduce the power
entering the fiber and give $\tilde\eta^{(1)}=\eta^{(1)}A_{\mathrm{pre}}^2$ referred to the span input.

\Cref{fig:gn-launch} checks the model against the split-step solver on the \emph{WDM with fiber
nonlinearity} scenario: five 32\,GBd DP-QPSK channels at 50\,GHz over $10\times80$\,km of standard fiber. The
markers are the MER of the centre channel after the full DSP chain; the line is the GSNR of
\cref{eq:gn} plus ASE ($\eta=\gneta$\,W$^{-2}$ per span, $P_{\mathrm{opt}}=\gnpopt$\,dBm). Both show the optimum launch
power and the $-2$\,dB/dB slope above it; the simulated optimum is within 1\,dB of the predicted one. Near
the optimum the simulation is about 1\,dB below the model because the MER includes the adaptive
equalizer and phase recovery penalties; at high power it is about 1\,dB above, because the GN model
overestimates the NLI of non-Gaussian formats such as QPSK (the correction of the enhanced GN
model~\cite{carena2014}).

\subsection{Non-identical spans and the LOGO principle}
Terrestrial links have spans of different lengths and fibers, and the WDM comb can change along the
route. With span $i$ described by its input power $P^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{ch},i}$, lumped losses $A_{\mathrm{pre},i}$, $A_{\mathrm{post},i}$,
fiber loss $A_{F,i}=e^{-2\alpha_iL_i}$ and amplifier $G_i$, $F_i$, the noise at the receiver is
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{ASE,tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^MP^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE},i}\prod_{j=i+1}^MA_{\mathrm{span},j}G_j,\qquad
  P_{\mathrm{NLI,tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^MP^{(1)}_{\mathrm{NLI},i}\prod_{j=i+1}^MA_{\mathrm{span},j}G_j,
\end{equation}
with $P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE},i}=hfBF_i(G_i-1)$ and $P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{NLI},i}=\tilde\eta_i(P^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{ch},i})^3A_{F,i}A_{\mathrm{post},i}G_i$. With transparency the
products are 1 and $\mathrm{GSNR}=P_{\mathrm{ch}}/\big(\sum_ihfBF_i(G_i-1)+P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3\sum_i\tilde\eta_i\big)$, but transparency is not
optimal for different spans. The total GSNR is the "parallel" combination of span-local GSNRs, each the
signal at the span output over the noise the span adds:
\begin{equation}
  \frac1{\mathrm{GSNR}_{\mathrm{tot}}}=\sum_{i=1}^M\frac1{\mathrm{GSNR}_i},\qquad
  \mathrm{GSNR}_i=\frac{P^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{ch},i}A_{\mathrm{span},i}G_i}{P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE},i}+\tilde\eta_i(P^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{ch},i})^3} .
\end{equation}
The \emph{LOGO} principle (local optimization, global optimization) follows: maximizing each $\mathrm{GSNR}_i$
maximizes the total, with
\begin{equation}
  P^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{ch},i,\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt[3]{\frac{P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE},i}}{2\tilde\eta_i}},\qquad
  \mathrm{GSNR}_{i,\max}=\frac{2A_{\mathrm{span},i}G_i}{3\sqrt[3]{2\tilde\eta_i\big[P^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ASE},i}\big]^2}} .
\end{equation}
The apparent circularity (the optimum
input power depends on the gain, which sets the next input power) disappears for $G_i\gg1$: the
optimum powers follow from the span properties, and the gains are then set to deliver them. The
3\,dB rule holds span by span. LOGO makes the management of large heterogeneous networks tractable.
For arbitrary combs the NLI on a channel splits into self-channel interference (SCI, related to SPM)
and cross-channel interference (XCI, XPM and FWM), given by the EGN model.

\begin{incode}
The split-step solver computes the NLI directly. For EDFA-only transparent links,
\blk{optical_link} also reports the GN estimate for the WDM comb in the \meta{wdm} metadata
(\code{gn_nli_dbm}, \code{gsnr_gn_db}, \code{gn_optimum_launch_dbm}), useful to choose the launch power
before running the heavy nonlinear simulation.
\end{incode}

\section{Wavelength-division multiplexing}
WDM is frequency-division multiplexing of optical carriers: each channel has its own laser and the
channels share the fiber and the amplifiers. In the C band (about 191.7--196.1\,THz) a 50\,GHz grid holds
about 100 channels, an 87.5\,GHz grid about 60. Optical filters select channels with a transfer function
on the field, $E_{\mathrm{out}}(f)=H_{\mathrm{opt}}(f)E_{\mathrm{in}}(f)$, as rectangular as possible: low loss in band, strong
rejection outside. A guard band is needed for the filters (wavelength-selective switches in
reconfigurable networks), which is why Nyquist WDM ($\delta_f\to0$) is hard to reach.

\blk{wdm_multiplexer} surrounds the channel under test with $N_{\mathrm{ch}}-1$ neighbours at multiples of the
spacing, carrying independent symbols with the same constellation, pulse shape and power.
Propagating the composite field through a nonlinear fiber produces cross-phase modulation and
four-wave mixing. \blk{wdm_demultiplexer} selects a channel with a second-order super-Gaussian filter,
$|H(f)|=\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{\ln2}{2}(2f/B)^4\big)$, 3\,dB down at $\pm B/2$. The sample rate must cover all channels.
\codeexcerpt{wdm_demux}{Channel selection by frequency shift and optical filtering.}

\section{Free-space optics}
\subsection{Scintillation}
Atmospheric turbulence makes the received intensity fluctuate (scintillation). \blk{fso_turbulence}
multiplies the field by $\sqrt h$, where the normalized irradiance $h$ ($\E h=1$) is constant
over a frame and follows one of two models.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Log-normal} (weak turbulence): $\ln h\sim\N(-\sigma^2/2,\sigma^2)$.
  \item \textbf{Gamma-gamma} (moderate to strong turbulence): $h$ is the product of two
  independent unit-mean gamma variables with parameters $\alpha$ (large eddies) and $\beta$
  (small eddies). Its density is
  \begin{equation}
    f(h)=\frac{2(\alpha\beta)^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,h^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}-1}K_{\alpha-\beta}\big(2\sqrt{\alpha\beta h}\big).
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

The parameters follow from the turbulence strength. For a plane wave the Rytov variance is
$\sigma_R^2=1.23\,C_n^2k^{7/6}L^{11/6}$, with $C_n^2$ the refractive-index structure parameter
(about $10^{-16}$ to $10^{-13}$\,m$^{-2/3}$ at optical wavelengths~\cite{p1814}). Al-Habash, Andrews and
Phillips~\cite{alhabash2001} give
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=\Big[e^{0.49\sigma_R^2/(1+1.11\sigma_R^{12/5})^{7/6}}-1\Big]^{-1},\qquad
  \beta=\Big[e^{0.51\sigma_R^2/(1+0.69\sigma_R^{12/5})^{5/6}}-1\Big]^{-1}.
\end{equation}
For example, $\sigma_R^2=1$ gives $\alpha=4.39$ and $\beta=2.56$. The scintillation index of the
gamma-gamma model is $\sigma_I^2=1/\alpha+1/\beta+1/(\alpha\beta)$. With \prm{rytov_variance} set,
\blk{fso_turbulence} derives $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and the log-normal variance
$\ln(1+\sigma_I^2)$, so both models share the same scintillation index. In weak turbulence that
index tends to $\sigma_R^2$.

\subsection{The complete link: \blk{fso_channel}}
\blk{fso_channel} computes the whole budget of ITU-R P.1814~\cite{p1814} from geometry and weather.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Atmospheric attenuation} from the visibility $V$ (2\,\% contrast threshold, km),
  with Kim's size-distribution exponent:
  \begin{equation}
    \gamma_{\mathrm{sp}}=\frac{17}{V}\Big(\frac{0.55\,\mu\mathrm m}{\lambda}\Big)^{q}\ \mathrm{dB/km},\qquad
    q=\begin{cases}1.6 & V>50\\ 1.3 & 6<V\le50\\ 0.16V+0.34 & 1\le V\le6\\ V-0.5 & 0.5\le V<1\\ 0 & V<0.5 .\end{cases}
  \end{equation}
  Light fog ($V=1$\,km) costs 10\,dB/km at 1550\,nm; haze at $V=5$\,km about 1\,dB/km.
  \item \textbf{Geometric loss}: the beam of full divergence $\theta$ covers the area
  $\frac\pi4(d\theta)^2$, so a receiver of diameter $D_R$ collects
  $A_{\mathrm{geo}}=20\log_{10}(d\theta/D_R)$\,dB, or 0 when the aperture catches the whole beam.
  \item \textbf{Scintillation} from $C_n^2$ via $\sigma_R^2$, as above.
  \item \textbf{Pointing errors}~\cite{farid2007}. Building sway gives a radial beam offset
  $r\sim\mathrm{Rayleigh}(\sigma_s)$ on the receiver plane. A Gaussian beam of radius
  $w_z=d\theta/2$ on an aperture of radius $a$ then collects
  $h_p=e^{-2r^2/w_{z,\mathrm{eq}}^2}$ relative to perfect alignment, with
  $w_{z,\mathrm{eq}}^2=w_z^2\sqrt\pi\,\mathrm{erf}(v)/(2v\,e^{-v^2})$ and $v=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,a/w_z$. On
  average $\E h_p=w_{z,\mathrm{eq}}^2/(w_{z,\mathrm{eq}}^2+4\sigma_s^2)$, which the test suite checks.
\end{itemize}
All fading terms are quasi-static (one value per frame) and appear on \code{csi}. The metrics give
each loss term and $\sigma_R^2$. Aperture averaging, beam wander and time-correlated fading are not
modelled. The \emph{Free-space optical link budget} scenario runs a 1\,Gb/s OOK link over 1\,km
with all four effects.
